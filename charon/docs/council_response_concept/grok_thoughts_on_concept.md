### Where it could go wrong (the realistic hazards)
A few currents could swamp the boat if ignored:

- **Distance metric & normalization matter.** Raw Euclidean or cosine on raw coefficients will probably produce noise. Ramanujan-Petersson bounds and conductor size mean you must normalize (e.g., divide by \(p^{(k-1)/2}\) for weight-\(k\) forms). p-adic valuations or congruence conditions might capture "arithmetic closeness" better than L2 in some regimes. The quality gates (known pairs must cluster, families must cohere) will catch gross failures, but you'll iterate on the metric early.

- **Scale & non-linearity.** Half a million objects is manageable with k-NN graphs and approximate nearest neighbors, but full pairwise distances aren't. Spectral embedding assumes roughly Euclidean geometry; if the true arithmetic manifold is highly curved or non-linear, you may later want UMAP, diffusion maps, or even graph neural nets. Incremental re-embedding (not full recompute every loop) will become essential once the landscape stabilizes.

- **Truncation & completeness.** First-50-primes is fine for low-conductor objects but can create artificial collisions at high conductors. Track coefficient_completeness explicitly (you already plan to) and treat incomplete vectors as "fuzzy points" until more data arrives.

None of these are show-stoppers—they're exactly what the HITL gates and failure classification are for. Your three-stage quality testing (correctness, completeness, geometric coherence) plus the recovery/family/exploration tests are robust.
