# Stability of Kronecker Coefficients (Murnaghan-Style)

### Key Points
- **Kronecker Coefficients:** These are fundamental constants in the representation theory of the symmetric group and algebraic combinatorics, defined as the multiplicities of irreducible representations in the tensor product of two other irreducible representations.
- **Murnaghan Stability:** Discovered in 1938 by Francis D. Murnaghan, this property demonstrates that when the first parts (the first rows of the Young diagrams) of the three indexing partitions are uniformly incremented, the corresponding Kronecker coefficients eventually become constant. 
- **Reduced Kronecker Coefficients:** The limit values of these stabilizing sequences are known as reduced (or stable) Kronecker coefficients. They play a significant role in geometric complexity theory and serve as structure constants for character polynomials.
- **Generalized and Hook Stability:** Recent breakthroughs have massively expanded on Murnaghan's work. John Stembridge formalized the concept of "stable triples," a conjecture later proven by Sam and Snowden using invariant theory. Concurrently, Briand, Rattan, and Rosas identified "hook stability," where cells are simultaneously added to the first row and first column.
- **Complexity and Saturation:** Pak and Panova proved that computing reduced Kronecker coefficients is strongly #P-hard and famously disproved the long-standing saturation conjecture for reduced Kronecker coefficients.
- **Heisenberg Coefficients:** The stability phenomenon has been generalized beyond the Kronecker product to the Heisenberg product of symmetric functions, demonstrating that the structural properties of Murnaghan stability are deeply embedded in broader algebraic frameworks.

### What are Kronecker Coefficients?
In mathematics, particularly in group theory, the symmetric group describes all the ways to permute a set of objects. The "irreducible representations" are the fundamental building blocks of the group's linear actions. When you take two such building blocks and combine them using a tensor product, the result can be broken down into a sum of these fundamental blocks. The number of times a specific block appears in this breakdown is called a Kronecker coefficient. Unlike the well-understood Littlewood-Richardson coefficients which arise in a related context, Kronecker coefficients lack a simple combinatorial formula, making them notoriously difficult to compute and a major subject of modern research.

### Why Does Stability Matter?
Stability implies that if you look at a sequence of Kronecker coefficients generated by making the underlying partitions larger in a specific, uniform way, the coefficients eventually stop changing. They hit a "stable" limit. This is profound because it means that to understand infinitely large complex structures, mathematicians only need to compute the values up to the point of stabilization. This limit value—the reduced Kronecker coefficient—is mathematically cleaner and better behaved than the standard coefficients, offering a backdoor to solving complex algorithmic and geometric problems.

### What is the Current State of Research?
For decades, Murnaghan's 1938 theorem was an isolated beautiful result. However, the 21st century has seen an explosion of interest due to connections with quantum information theory and computational complexity (like the P vs. NP problem). Researchers have found that stability is not limited to adding boxes to the first row of a partition; it applies to "hooks" (rows and columns) and general "stable triples." Furthermore, the exact computational difficulty of finding these stable values has been pinned down: they are extremely hard to compute (#P-hard), meaning no fast algorithms are likely to exist, challenging previous hopes that reduced coefficients might offer easy computational shortcuts.

## 1. Introduction and Preliminaries

The study of Kronecker coefficients resides at the nexus of algebraic combinatorics, representation theory of the symmetric and general linear groups, and computational complexity theory. Let \( n \) be a non-negative integer, and let \( \lambda \vdash n \) denote that \( \lambda \) is an integer partition of \( n \), represented as a weakly decreasing sequence of non-negative integers \( \lambda = (\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_k) \) such that \( \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i = n \).

To each partition \( \lambda \vdash n \), one associates an irreducible representation of the symmetric group \( S_n \), denoted as \( V_\lambda \) (or sometimes \( S^\lambda \) or \( I_\lambda \)). The representation theory of the symmetric group over the complex numbers is semisimple, meaning any finite-dimensional representation can be decomposed into a direct sum of these irreducible representations. 

When one considers the tensor (or Kronecker) product of two irreducible representations \( V_\lambda \) and \( V_\mu \) of the same symmetric group \( S_n \), the resulting module \( V_\lambda \otimes V_\mu \) is also a representation of \( S_n \) (via the diagonal action). Because of semisimplicity, it decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible representations \( V_\nu \):
\[ V_\lambda \otimes V_\mu \cong \bigoplus_{\nu \vdash n} V_\nu^{\oplus g(\lambda, \mu, \nu)} \]
The multiplicities \( g(\lambda, \mu, \nu) \) are non-negative integers called the **Kronecker coefficients** [cite: 1, 2]. 

Equivalently, via the Frobenius characteristic map, the representation theory of \( S_n \) is isomorphic to the ring of symmetric functions. Under this isomorphism, the irreducible representation \( V_\lambda \) maps to the Schur function \( s_\lambda \). The tensor product of representations corresponds to an operation on symmetric functions known as the **Kronecker product**, denoted by \( * \). Thus, the Kronecker coefficients can be defined as the structure constants of the Kronecker product of Schur functions [cite: 3, 4]:
\[ s_\lambda * s_\mu = \sum_{\nu \vdash n} g(\lambda, \mu, \nu) s_\nu \]

Despite nearly a century of rigorous research since their formal introduction by Francis D. Murnaghan in 1938 [cite: 2, 5], the Kronecker coefficients remain largely mysterious. They are viewed as a highly complex "big cousin" to the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients \( c_{\mu, \nu}^\lambda \), which describe the induction product of symmetric groups and the tensor product of general linear group representations [cite: 2, 6]. While the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients enjoy a strictly combinatorial description (counting specific types of Young tableaux), no such generally applicable combinatorial rule (like a "Littlewood-Richardson rule") is known for the Kronecker coefficients [cite: 7, 8]. This absence severely limits our ability to evaluate, estimate, or determine the positivity of Kronecker coefficients in general cases.

However, a fundamental and deeply structural property of these coefficients was observed early on: they exhibit specific stabilization phenomena when the indexing partitions are allowed to grow in predefined patterns. The most classical of these is **Murnaghan's stability**, which paved the way for modern investigations into reduced Kronecker coefficients, representation stability, and computational complexity.

## 2. The Phenomenon of Kronecker Stability: Murnaghan's Theorem

### 2.1. Historical Context and Murnaghan's Original Observation
In a seminal paper published in 1938, Francis D. Murnaghan discovered a remarkable stabilization property of the Kronecker coefficients [cite: 3, 5]. He observed that when computing the Kronecker product of two Schur functions, if one progressively increases the size of the first part of the indexing partitions, the sequence of structural coefficients eventually stabilizes. 

To formalize this, we introduce the standard notation for adjoining a large first row to a partition. Let \( \alpha = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots) \) be a partition of some integer \( |\alpha| \). For any integer \( n \ge |\alpha| + \alpha_1 \), we define a new partition \( \alpha[n] \) of size \( n \) by prepending a new first part to \( \alpha \):
\[ \alpha[n] = (n - |\alpha|, \alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots) \]
This sequence \( \alpha[n] \) is guaranteed to be a valid partition (i.e., weakly decreasing) precisely because the condition \( n - |\alpha| \ge \alpha_1 \) ensures that the new first part is at least as large as the original first part \( \alpha_1 \) [cite: 9, 10].

Murnaghan's Theorem states that for any three fixed partitions \( \alpha, \beta, \gamma \), the sequence of Kronecker coefficients 
\[ g(\alpha[n], \beta[n], \gamma[n]) \]
is eventually constant for sufficiently large values of \( n \) [cite: 7, 11]. This means that the multiplicity of the representation \( V_{\gamma[n]} \) in \( V_{\alpha[n]} \otimes V_{\beta[n]} \) does not depend on \( n \) once \( n \) exceeds a certain threshold.

### 2.2. The Reduced Kronecker Coefficients
Because the sequence stabilizes, it is mathematically rigorous to define the limit, which is known as the **reduced Kronecker coefficient** (or stable Kronecker coefficient), denoted typically by \( \bar{g}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) \) [cite: 10, 12]:
\[ \bar{g}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = \lim_{n \to \infty} g(\alpha[n], \beta[n], \gamma[n]) \]
Unlike ordinary Kronecker coefficients, which are only defined for triples of partitions that partition the same integer \( n \) (i.e., \( |\alpha| = |\beta| = |\gamma| = n \)), the reduced Kronecker coefficients are well-defined for *any* triple of arbitrary partitions \( \alpha, \beta, \gamma \), regardless of their respective weights [cite: 11, 13]. 

Murnaghan's theorem also provides necessary conditions for these reduced coefficients to be non-zero. Specifically, the reduced Kronecker coefficient \( \bar{g}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) \) vanishes unless the weights of the three partitions satisfy the triangle inequalities [cite: 9]:
\[ |\alpha| \le |\beta| + |\gamma|, \quad |\beta| \le |\alpha| + |\gamma|, \quad |\gamma| \le |\alpha| + |\beta| \]
These are commonly referred to as Murnaghan's inequalities [cite: 9]. 

The reduced Kronecker coefficients have grown into objects of intense independent study. They are the structure constants for the ring of character polynomials [cite: 5, 14]. As noted by Briand, Orellana, and Rosas, the ordinary Kronecker coefficients can actually be expressed as a small alternating sum of reduced Kronecker coefficients, and conversely, reduced Kronecker coefficients can be expressed as certain sums of ordinary Kronecker coefficients evaluated on smaller partitions [cite: 5, 14]. This interplay suggests that the reduced coefficients contain the fundamental "DNA" of the tensor products of the symmetric group.

### 2.3. Bounds for Stabilization and the Stability Index
A critical question in the study of Murnaghan stability is determining the exact point at which the sequence becomes constant. We denote by \( \text{stab}(\alpha, \beta) \) the smallest integer \( N \) such that for all \( n \ge N \), the expansion of the Kronecker product \( s_{\alpha[n]} * s_{\beta[n]} \) stabilizes [cite: 3, 15]. 

Briand, Orellana, and Rosas provided an explicit formula for this stabilization bound. They proved that for any two partitions \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \), the global stability index is given by:
\[ \text{stab}(\alpha, \beta) = |\alpha| + |\beta| + \alpha_1 + \beta_1 \]
This implies that for any \( n \ge |\alpha| + |\beta| + \alpha_1 + \beta_1 \), no new terms are introduced in the Kronecker product, and the coefficients remain fixed at their stable limits [cite: 3]. For example, if \( \alpha = (6, 4, 3) \) and \( \beta = (5, 3, 3) \), then the stabilization occurs precisely at \( 13 + 11 + 6 + 5 = 35 \) [cite: 3]. 

This explicit determination of the bound shifted the perspective on Kronecker stability from an abstract limit theorem to an effectively computable threshold, facilitating algorithmic approaches to generating symmetric functions and studying their structural limits.

## 3. Generalized Stability and Stembridge's Framework

### 3.1. Beyond the First Row
For a long time, Murnaghan stability was viewed primarily through the lens of adding cells to the first row of a partition. However, in 2014, John Stembridge proposed a vastly more general framework for stability that does not arbitrarily privilege the first row [cite: 1, 16]. Stembridge asked whether stability phenomena occur in limits along other discrete rays in the space of partitions. 

To explore this, Stembridge considered sequences of Kronecker coefficients where all parts of the partitions are incremented simultaneously by scalar multiples of fixed partitions. Let \( \alpha, \beta, \gamma \) be a triple of partitions (which can be viewed as integer vectors). Stembridge defined a sequence of Kronecker coefficients by considering base partitions \( \lambda, \mu, \nu \) and translating them by \( n \) times the components of the triple \( (\alpha, \beta, \gamma) \):
\[ g(\lambda + n\alpha, \mu + n\beta, \nu + n\gamma) \]
In this notation, addition of partitions is done component-wise (as vectors). Murnaghan's classical stability is cleanly recovered in this notation by setting \( \alpha = \beta = \gamma = (1) \) (the partition consisting of a single box) [cite: 7]. 

### 3.2. Stable Triples and Stembridge's Conjecture
Stembridge formalized the concept of a **stable triple**. A triple of partitions \( (\alpha, \beta, \gamma) \) is called a *stable triple* if, for every choice of base partitions \( (\lambda, \mu, \nu) \), the sequence 
\[ g(\lambda + n\alpha, \mu + n\beta, \nu + n\gamma) \]
is bounded for all \( n \ge 1 \) (which, since they are integers, means the sequence is eventually constant or convergent) [cite: 1]. 

Stembridge formulated a striking and elegant conjecture to classify all such stable triples. He hypothesized that a triple \( (\alpha, \beta, \gamma) \) is stable if and only if the corresponding Kronecker coefficients for the ray itself are trivially equal to one for all points along the ray:
\[ g(d\alpha, d\beta, d\gamma) = 1 \quad \text{for all } d > 0 \]
This conjecture (Stembridge's Conjecture 4.3) effortlessly implies Murnaghan's original theorem. In Murnaghan's case, \( \alpha = \beta = \gamma = (1) \), and \( g((d), (d), (d)) = 1 \) for all \( d > 0 \) because the representation corresponding to the single-row partition \( (d) \) is the trivial representation of \( S_d \), and the tensor product of two trivial representations is naturally the trivial representation [cite: 17].

Stembridge proved the "only if" direction of this conjecture using combinatorial bounding techniques [cite: 17]. However, proving the reverse direction—that \( g(d\alpha, d\beta, d\gamma) = 1 \) guarantees global stability for all arbitrary base partitions \( \lambda, \mu, \nu \)—remained a significant challenge.

### 3.3. The Proof by Sam and Snowden
The full proof of Stembridge's conjecture was successfully achieved by Steven Sam and Andrew Snowden in 2015 [cite: 14, 17]. Their proof was notable because it completely bypassed traditional symmetric function manipulations and standard Kronecker coefficient identities, relying instead on sophisticated techniques from invariant theory, Schur-Weyl duality, and the Borel-Weil theorem [cite: 17]. 

Sam and Snowden translated the problem into the language of modules over finitely generated algebras. Specifically, they reinterpreted Murnaghan stability as a structural property: the Segre product of finitely generated FI-modules is itself finitely generated [cite: 17]. FI-modules are algebraic structures indexed by finite sets and injections, which have revolutionized the study of representation stability in algebraic topology and group theory.

By translating the Kronecker coefficients into Hilbert functions of modules over finitely generated twisted commutative algebras (TCAs), Sam and Snowden were able to show that if \( g(d\alpha, d\beta, d\gamma) = 1 \) for all \( d > 0 \), the underlying algebra is essentially a domain of degree one. They proved that the rational singularities property of the rings involved forces the dimensions of the graded components to eventually become constant, thereby establishing that the sequence \( g(\lambda + n\alpha, \mu + n\beta, \nu + n\gamma) \) stabilizes for \( n \gg 0 \) [cite: 17]. 

This monumental proof not only verified Stembridge's conjecture but permanently embedded Kronecker coefficient stability into the broader landscape of Representation Stability Theory.

## 4. Hook Stability and Structural Expansions

While Stembridge's generalization focused on vector-wise addition of parts, Briand, Rattan, and Rosas (2017) discovered a new, distinct form of stability that alters the shape of the partitions in a non-linear vector manner. This phenomenon is known as **Hook Stability** [cite: 7].

### 4.1. Extending to the First Column
In Murnaghan stability, one adds cells exclusively to the first row of a partition. Briand, Rattan, and Rosas investigated what happens if one adds cells to *both* the first row and the first column simultaneously [cite: 11]. 

Let \( \lambda \) be a non-empty partition. They defined a "hook addition" operation denoted by \( \lambda \oplus (a|b) \), which constructs a new partition by adding \( a \) cells to the first row of \( \lambda \) and \( b \) cells to the first column. Formally, this is defined as \( \lambda + (a) \cup (1^b) \) [cite: 7]. For example, if \( \lambda = (8, 3, 3, 1) \), then \( \lambda \oplus (7|4) = (15, 3, 3, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1) \) [cite: 7]. This operation effectively expands the main hook of the Young diagram.

### 4.2. Hook Stability Phenomenon
The authors proved that sequences of Kronecker coefficients exhibit stabilization under this balanced hook expansion. Specifically, if one increments the first row and the first column of the three indexing partitions simultaneously, the Kronecker coefficients stabilize [cite: 8, 18].

They achieved this by first establishing properties for families of reduced Kronecker coefficients and then translating these properties back to the ordinary Kronecker coefficients. A remarkable feature of hook stability is that it does not fit straightforwardly into the standard representation theory of fixed general linear groups, as it inherently involves sequences of Kronecker coefficients indexed by partitions with an unbounded number of parts (since the lengths of the columns are growing towards infinity) [cite: 8, 11]. 

To illustrate, consider the Kronecker coefficients \( g((3,3) \oplus (i|j), (3,3) \oplus (i|j), (3,3) \oplus (i|j)) \). The authors demonstrated that such a matrix of coefficients contains regions that are highly stable. The columns stabilize due to classical Murnaghan stability (growing rows), while the rows eventually vanish due to length constraints. However, there exists a distinct "hook stable" region in the interior where the values remain non-zero and perfectly constant (e.g., stabilizing at the value 145 for specific choices of \( i \) and \( j \)) [cite: 7]. 

### 4.3. Linear Growth and Asymptotic Estimates
In addition to hook stability, Briand, Rattan, and Rosas analyzed what occurs when one increases the sizes of the first *two* rows of the three indexing partitions in a specified balanced manner. They proved that in this scenario, rather than remaining bounded and stabilizing, the Kronecker coefficients grow *linearly* [cite: 7, 8].

This was a major breakthrough in understanding the asymptotic behavior of Kronecker coefficients. By viewing the coefficients through the lens of symmetric function generation and utilizing vertex operators, they were able to give precise asymptotic estimates for this linear growth, bridging the gap between exact algebraic constants and asymptotic combinatorial geometry [cite: 7].

## 5. Superstability in Restricted Cases

The stability of Kronecker coefficients has been studied deeply in specific sub-families of partitions, yielding even stronger structural results. Ballantine and Hallahan (2016) investigated the Kronecker product of two Schur functions where one is indexed by a **hook partition** (a partition of the form \( (n-k, 1^k) \)) and the other by a **rectangle partition** (a partition where all rows have the same length, e.g., \( (c^r) \)) [cite: 4]. 

### 5.1. The Superstability Property
Building on the work of Jonah Blasiak, Ballantine and Hallahan proved not just a stability result, but a phenomenon they termed **superstability** [cite: 4]. 

Stability generally implies that the *multiplicity* (the coefficient) of a specific Schur function in the tensor product decomposition remains constant. Superstability is a far stronger condition: it dictates that beyond a certain sharp bound, the actual *support* of the decomposition freezes. That is, once the bound is reached, absolutely no new Schur functions appear in the decomposition of the Kronecker product [cite: 4]. 

If one expands \( s_{\alpha[n]} * s_{\beta[n]} \), stability says the coefficient of \( s_{\gamma[n]} \) is constant. Superstability says that for any partition \( \gamma \) that could theoretically appear, if it hasn't appeared by the superstability bound, its coefficient will remain zero forever [cite: 4]. 

### 5.2. Sharp Bounds for Recovery
For the product of a hook and a rectangle partition, Ballantine and Hallahan provided nearly sharp, explicit algebraic bounds for the size of the partition starting at which superstability holds [cite: 4]. Remarkably, their bound depends only on two parameters of the partitions (the size of the hook and the dimensions of the rectangle), rather than on the specific weights of all three partitions, which was a limitation in earlier bounding formulas by Vallejo and others [cite: 4]. 

The existence of superstability means that one can perfectly recover the complete Schur decomposition of an infinitely large Kronecker product simply by computing the smallest case in which the superstability bound is met [cite: 4]. This offers massive computational shortcuts for applications in physics, as the Kronecker products of hook and rectangle shapes are heavily utilized in the theoretical understanding of the quantum Hall effect [cite: 4].

## 6. Computational Complexity and the Saturation Property

One of the driving forces behind the modern renaissance in Kronecker coefficient research is Geometric Complexity Theory (GCT), a program initiated by Mulmuley and Sohoni that attempts to separate complexity classes (such as P vs. NP) using representation theory and algebraic geometry. Within GCT, understanding the computational difficulty of Kronecker coefficients is paramount.

### 6.1. The Complexity of Kronecker Coefficients
It is a well-known result by Ikenmeyer, Mulmuley, and Walter (2017) that computing the ordinary Kronecker coefficient \( g(\lambda, \mu, \nu) \) is **#P-hard** [cite: 10, 13]. Even more severely, it is *strongly #P-hard*, meaning the problem remains #P-hard even when the input partitions \( (\lambda, \mu, \nu) \) are given in unary representation (i.e., the input size is the total number of boxes in the Young diagrams) [cite: 10]. 

Furthermore, the related problem of merely deciding whether a Kronecker coefficient is strictly positive (i.e., determining if \( g(\lambda, \mu, \nu) > 0 \)) is known to be NP-hard [cite: 13]. This stands in stark contrast to the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients, where deciding positivity is in the complexity class P, and computing the actual values is #P-complete but well-understood via combinatorial rules [cite: 13].

### 6.2. The Hardness of Reduced Kronecker Coefficients
For years, it was hypothesized that because reduced Kronecker coefficients \( \bar{g}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) \) are stable limits, they might represent a "smoother," more mathematically tractable version of the ordinary coefficients. There was hope that they could be computed more efficiently.

This hope was definitively dismantled by Igor Pak and Greta Panova in 2020. They proved that **computing the reduced Kronecker coefficients is also strongly #P-hard** under parsimonious many-one reductions [cite: 10, 13]. By providing an explicit isomorphism of the corresponding highest weight vector spaces, they showed that the hardness of the ordinary coefficients translates directly to the reduced coefficients. 

Pak and Panova also settled a major open question regarding positivity: they proved that deciding whether a reduced Kronecker coefficient is positive (i.e., \( \bar{g}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) > 0 \)) is **NP-hard** [cite: 13]. This establishes that the reduced coefficients are intrinsically just as intractable as their ordinary counterparts, closing off certain theoretical avenues in GCT.

### 6.3. Disproving the Saturation Property
A pivotal mathematical concept in this area is the **saturation property**. A family of coefficients is said to satisfy the saturation property if the positivity of a scaled version implies the positivity of the unscaled version. For Littlewood-Richardson coefficients, the famous Saturation Theorem (proven by Knutson and Tao in 1999) states that if \( c_{N\mu, N\nu}^{N\lambda} > 0 \) for some integer \( N \ge 1 \), then \( c_{\mu, \nu}^\lambda > 0 \) [cite: 19]. 

It has long been known that ordinary Kronecker coefficients *do not* satisfy the saturation property. A standard counterexample is that \( g(2^2, 2^2, 2^2) = 1 \) (where \( 2^2 \) denotes the partition \( (2,2) \)), but \( g(1^2, 1^2, 1^2) = 0 \) [cite: 2, 10, 13]. 

Because the reduced Kronecker coefficients are viewed as interpolations between Littlewood-Richardson and ordinary Kronecker coefficients, Kirillov (2004) and Klyachko (2004) independently conjectured that the reduced Kronecker coefficients *do* possess the saturation property. That is, they conjectured that if \( \bar{g}(N\alpha, N\beta, N\gamma) > 0 \) for some \( N > 0 \), then \( \bar{g}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) > 0 \) [cite: 10, 13].

In a stunning result, Pak and Panova explicitly **disproved the saturation conjecture for reduced Kronecker coefficients** [cite: 10, 13]. They provided a concrete, infinite family of counterexamples. They proved that for all integers \( k \ge 3 \), the triple of partitions:
\[ \alpha = (1^{k^2-1}), \quad \beta = (1^{k^2-1}), \quad \gamma = (k^{k-1}) \]
serves as a counterexample [cite: 10]. For these triples, there exists a scaling factor \( N \) where the reduced Kronecker coefficient is strictly positive, yet the base reduced coefficient evaluates to zero. This disproof definitively moved the reduced Kronecker coefficients away from the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients on the spectrum of algebraic behavior, confirming their wild and highly non-linear nature [cite: 13].

## 7. Stability of Heisenberg Coefficients

The stability phenomena discovered by Murnaghan is so fundamental that it extends beyond the standard Kronecker product to generalized algebraic structures. One such generalization involves the **Heisenberg product**.

### 7.1. The Heisenberg Product of Symmetric Functions
Introduced by Aguiar, Ferrer Santos, and Moreira, the Heisenberg product is a non-graded, associative product defined on the ring of symmetric functions (and correspondingly on representations of symmetric groups) [cite: 20, 21]. It is designed to algebraically interpolate between the ordinary induction product (which yields Littlewood-Richardson coefficients) and the Kronecker product [cite: 20, 21].

Because it interpolates between these two operations, when the Heisenberg product is applied to two Schur functions, the resulting expansion yields terms of varying degrees. The highest degree component strictly matches the classical induction product, while the lowest degree components (when the Schur functions have the same degree) match the Kronecker product [cite: 21]. 

The structure constants for this product are called **Heisenberg coefficients** [cite: 20]. They naturally generalize both the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients and the Kronecker coefficients, embedding both within a single algebraic framework.

### 7.2. Murnaghan-Style Stability for Heisenberg Coefficients
Given that the lowest degree component of the Heisenberg product mirrors the Kronecker product, a natural line of inquiry was whether the Heisenberg product as a whole inherits Murnaghan stability. This was definitively answered by Li Ying (2019), who proved an analogous stabilization result for the Heisenberg product of Schur functions [cite: 20, 21]. 

Ying showed that *each degree component* of the Heisenberg product stabilizes in the exact same sense as Murnaghan's theorem [cite: 21]. If one takes the Heisenberg product of \( s_{\lambda[n]} \) and \( s_{\mu[n]} \), the coefficients of the resulting expansion become entirely independent of \( n \) for sufficiently large \( n \) [cite: 21]. 

Furthermore, Ying generalized Stembridge's concept of stable triples to the Heisenberg realm. By leveraging the previous characterizations of stable triples by Sam and Snowden, Ying proved that any stable triple for either Kronecker coefficients or Littlewood-Richardson coefficients inherently stabilizes the Heisenberg coefficients [cite: 20]. Utilizing ideas involving additive matrices pioneered by Vallejo and Manivel, Ying provided mechanisms to classify and generate these Heisenberg stable triples [cite: 20]. This highlights that Murnaghan stability is not an isolated quirk of the Kronecker product, but a universal geometric and algebraic property of limits within symmetric function theory.

## 8. Asymptotics and Bounds on Kronecker Coefficients

Understanding the scale and growth rate of Kronecker coefficients is just as important as understanding their stability. As partitions grow large, how rapidly do the Kronecker coefficients inflate? 

Pak and Panova conducted extensive research into the asymptotic behavior and upper bounds of Kronecker coefficients, particularly for partitions with restricted shapes [cite: 22, 23]. A major open problem in the field was confirming whether Kronecker coefficients grow polynomially or exponentially under certain constraints. 

For partitions with a bounded number of rows (e.g., \( \lambda, \mu, \nu \vdash n \) such that their lengths \( \ell(\lambda), \ell(\mu), \ell(\nu) \le k \)), Pak and Panova proved a strict polynomial upper bound. They confirmed a conjecture (Conjecture 1.3 in their work) that:
\[ g(\lambda, \mu, \nu) \le n^{c(k)} \]
for some explicit constant \( c(k) > 0 \) depending only on the number of rows \( k \), and entirely independent of \( n \) [cite: 22]. 

By carefully analyzing contingency arrays and applying symmetric function techniques, they provided the explicit polynomial bound:
\[ g(\lambda, \mu, \nu) \le \frac{1}{k!^{8k^2} 2^{8k^3}} n^{4k^3 + 13k^2 + 31k} \]
[cite: 22]. Conversely, they also established explicit lower bounds proving that their polynomial upper bound is tight up to a constant in the leading exponent term, demonstrating that there exist partitions where the Kronecker coefficient grows at least as fast as \( C_k n^{k^3 - 3k^2 - 3k + 3} \) [cite: 22]. 

Furthermore, in separate work analyzing partitions based on the size of their Durfee square, Pak and Panova resolved another major open problem. They proved that for partitions \( \lambda, \mu, \nu \) with a fixed Durfee square size, the Kronecker coefficients grow at most polynomially. However, for self-conjugate partitions (where \( \lambda = \lambda' \)), they showed that the maximal Kronecker coefficients \( g(\lambda, \lambda, \lambda) \) grow **superexponentially**, revealing the extreme variance in magnitude that Kronecker coefficients can exhibit depending on the symmetry of the indexing partitions [cite: 23].

## 9. Conclusion

The stability of Kronecker coefficients, first glimpsed by Francis D. Murnaghan in 1938, has evolved from a specific property of symmetric polynomials into a vast, multidimensional domain of modern mathematics. From Stembridge's stable triples and the categorical proofs by Sam and Snowden using FI-modules, to the geometric hook stability of Briand, Rattan, and Rosas, the structural limits of tensor product multiplicities reveal profound underlying regularities in representation theory. 

Yet, these limits do not offer an escape from computational intractability. The monumental results by Pak and Panova proving the strongly #P-hard nature of reduced Kronecker coefficients and dismantling the saturation conjecture demonstrate that even at their stable limits, Kronecker coefficients contain immense computational complexity. As research continues to probe these coefficients through generalized frameworks like the Heisenberg product and advanced asymptotic bounding, the "Murnaghan-style" stability remains the foundational bedrock upon which the future of algebraic combinatorics and geometric complexity theory is being built.
