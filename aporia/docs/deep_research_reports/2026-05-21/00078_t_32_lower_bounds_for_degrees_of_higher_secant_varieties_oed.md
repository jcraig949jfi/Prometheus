## Introduction to Secant Varieties and Tensor Decomposition

The study of secant varieties bridges the gap between classical algebraic geometry and modern computational mathematics. Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $k$ (typically assumed to be $\mathbb{C}$ of characteristic 0). A projective variety $X \subset \mathbb{P}(V)$ parameterizes a specific class of geometric objects. In the context of multilinear algebra, the ambient space is often a tensor product of vector spaces $V = V_1 \otimes V_2 \otimes \dots \otimes V_d$, and the variety $X$ is the **Segre variety**, which is the image of the Segre embedding:
\[ \text{Seg}: \mathbb{P}(V_1) \times \mathbb{P}(V_2) \times \dots \times \mathbb{P}(V_d) \to \mathbb{P}(V_1 \otimes V_2 \otimes \dots \otimes V_d) \]
The points on the Segre variety correspond to non-zero decomposable tensors, also known as rank-1 tensors [cite: 7]. These take the form $v_1 \otimes v_2 \otimes \dots \otimes v_d$. 

The **$r$-th secant variety**, denoted $\sigma_r(X)$, is defined geometrically as the Zariski closure of the union of all linear spaces spanned by $r$ points lying on $X$ [cite: 7]. Mathematically, it is expressed as:
\[ \sigma_r(X) := \overline{\bigcup_{p_1, \dots, p_r \in X} \text{span}(p_1, \dots, p_r)} \]
In the language of tensors, a point in $\sigma_r(X)$ represents a tensor whose **border rank** is at most $r$ [cite: 7]. The border rank of a tensor $T$ is the minimum $r$ such that $T$ can be expressed as the limit of a sequence of tensors, each of which is a sum of $r$ rank-1 tensors. Because taking the Zariski closure adds limit points that may not strictly be sums of $r$ rank-1 tensors, the border rank of a tensor can be strictly less than its exact tensor rank. 

Finding the defining ideal $I(\sigma_r(X))$—the set of all polynomial equations that vanish on the secant variety—is a central problem in algebraic geometry. These equations provide explicit, computationally verifiable criteria for determining lower bounds on the border rank of a given tensor. If a tensor $T$ does not satisfy the equations defining $\sigma_r(X)$, then $T \notin \sigma_r(X)$, proving definitively that the border rank of $T$ is strictly greater than $r$. 

## The Geometry of Segre Products and Identifiability

The expected dimension of a secant variety can be determined by a naive parameter count. Each of the $r$ points on the variety $X$ requires $\dim X$ parameters, and specifying a point in their $(r-1)$-dimensional linear span requires an additional $r-1$ parameters. Thus, the expected dimension is:
\[ \text{expdim}(\sigma_r(X)) = \min(N, r(\dim X + 1) - 1) \]
where $N$ is the dimension of the ambient projective space. If the actual dimension of $\sigma_r(X)$ is strictly less than the expected dimension, the variety is said to be **defective** [cite: 7]. 

The specific case studied by Luke Oeding and Steven V. Sam is the fifth secant variety of the Segre product of five copies of the projective line [cite: 1]. Let $V_i \cong \mathbb{C}^2$ for $i=1, \dots, 5$. The ambient space is $\mathbb{P}(V_1 \otimes V_2 \otimes V_3 \otimes V_4 \otimes V_5) \cong \mathbb{P}^{31}$. The dimension of the Segre variety $X = \text{Seg}((\mathbb{P}^1)^{\times 5})$ is 5.
For $r=5$, the expected dimension of the secant variety $\sigma_5(X)$ is:
\[ \min(31, 5(5 + 1) - 1) = \min(31, 29) = 29 \]
Because the expected dimension is 29 and the ambient space has dimension 31, the secant variety is expected to have a codimension of 2. It is known that this specific variety is not defective, meaning its actual dimension is indeed 29 [cite: 7].

However, this format ($2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2$) is highly mathematically significant due to its identifiability properties. A tensor is said to be $k$-identifiable if it has a unique decomposition into $k$ rank-1 tensors. A fundamental result by Bocci and Chiantini demonstrated that while tensors in this format are not uniquely identifiable in rank 5, the generic tensor of this format possesses exactly 2 distinct minimal decompositions [cite: 1]. For Segre products involving 6 or more factors, the variety is known to be $k$-identifiable for almost all possible values of $k$ below the generic rank [cite: 1]. The unique boundary behavior of the 5-factor binary secant variety in rank 5 makes it a prime candidate for a detailed geometric and algebraic case study.

## The Failure of Classical Flattenings

Historically, the most reliable method for generating equations of secant varieties is through matrix flattenings. If a tensor $A \in V_1 \otimes \dots \otimes V_d$ has tensor rank $r$, it can be reshaped into a matrix $F(A)$ (a flattening) whose row and column spaces are constructed by partitioning the vector spaces $V_i$ into two disjoint sets. The fundamental property of flattenings is that subadditivity of matrix rank implies that if $F(A)$ has rank $p$ when $A$ is a rank-1 tensor, then $F(A)$ has rank at most $r \cdot p$ when $A$ has rank $r$ [cite: 1, 5]. 

For standard flattenings ($p=1$), if $A$ has rank $r$, then any flattening $F(A)$ will have a matrix rank of at most $r$. Consequently, the $(r+1) \times (r+1)$ minors of the flattened matrix will vanish, providing defining equations for the secant variety [cite: 1]. 

However, in the Oeding-Sam case of $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensors, the standard flattening method fails completely for $r=5$. The vector space dimensions are $2^5 = 32$. The only possible matrix flattenings (up to transposition) are:
1. One vector space against four: A $2 \times 16$ matrix.
2. Two vector spaces against three: A $4 \times 8$ matrix.

The maximum possible ranks for these flattened matrices are 2 and 4, respectively [cite: 1, 5]. Because we are investigating the 5th secant variety, we would need to compute $(5+1) \times (5+1) = 6 \times 6$ minors to find equations that vanish on rank 5 tensors. Since a $4 \times 8$ matrix cannot possibly have a non-zero $6 \times 6$ minor, these classical flattenings provide absolutely no non-trivial equations for $\sigma_5((\mathbb{P}^1)^{\times 5})$ [cite: 1, 5]. Advanced techniques, such as Young flattenings and Koszul flattenings, also fall short of fully defining the ideal in this highly symmetric boundary case. This necessitates a search for specialized invariant polynomials.

## Exploiting Symmetry and Representation Theory

Recognizing the inadequacy of flattening techniques, Oeding and Sam turned to the extensive symmetry of the variety to guide their search for defining equations. The Segre variety, and by extension its secant varieties, is invariant under a natural group action. For $X = \sigma_5((\mathbb{P}^1)^{\times 5})$, the ambient vector space $V = (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes 5}$ is acted upon by the group $G = SL(2)^{\times 5} \rtimes \Sigma_5$, where $SL(2)$ acts by changing coordinates on each $\mathbb{C}^2$ factor, and the symmetric group $\Sigma_5$ acts by permuting the five factors [cite: 1].

The coordinate ring of the ambient projective space is the symmetric algebra $\text{Sym}(V^*)$. The action of $G$ on $V$ defines a degree-preserving linear action on $\text{Sym}(V^*)$ via pullback [cite: 8]. The defining ideal of the secant variety, $I(X)$, must therefore be a $G$-submodule of $\text{Sym}(V^*)$ [cite: 8]. This allows researchers to search for defining equations not by looking at arbitrary polynomials, but by searching specifically within the spaces of invariants and semi-invariants of $G$.

Oeding and Sam focused their search on the spaces of semi-invariants for $SL(2)^{\times 5} \rtimes \Sigma_5$. By calculating character theories and analyzing the irreducible representations, they pinpointed the exact degrees where such invariants must exist [cite: 1]. 

### The Degree 6 Equation ($f_6$)
Through their representation-theoretic analysis, the authors discovered a degree 6 invariant. Given a monomial in the coordinates of $\mathbb{P}(V)$, one can define its skew-symmetrization under the action of $\Sigma_5$. The polynomial $f_6$ was explicitly constructed as the sum of the skew-symmetrizations of 15 specific monomials [cite: 1]. This process generated a massive polynomial containing 864 distinct monomials [cite: 1]. Using the classical symbolic method, the authors unconditionally proved that a general point of the fifth secant variety evaluates to zero under this invariant, definitively placing $f_6$ inside the ideal $I(X)$ [cite: 1]. 

### The Degree 16 Equation ($f_{16}$)
Finding the second equation required a blend of symbolic symmetry and numerical computation. By analyzing the dimensions of the spaces of $\Sigma_5$-invariants, Oeding and Sam identified that a degree 16 equation, $f_{16}$, was required to complete the intersection. This polynomial was constructed explicitly to be a $\Sigma_5$-invariant [cite: 1]. 

However, unlike $f_6$, unconditionally proving that $f_{16}$ vanishes on the entirety of $X$ through symbolic manipulation was computationally prohibitive due to the astronomical number of terms. Instead, the authors verified its vanishing by evaluating it on a large collection of pseudo-random points sampled directly from $X$ [cite: 1]. To ensure the robustness of this test, they selected 39 pseudo-random points and computed the evaluation matrix, verifying that it had full rank and establishing the independence of the evaluations [cite: 1]. Although these evaluations were computationally expensive—taking approximately 2 to 3 days of distributed computational time on robust servers—they provided a rigorous probabilistic guarantee that $f_{16}$ belongs to the ideal of $X$ "with high probability" [cite: 1].

## Lower Bounds for the Algebraic Degree: Lemma 3.2

To definitively prove that the ideal of $X$ is generated exactly and minimally by $f_6$ and $f_{16}$, Oeding and Sam needed to establish the algebraic degree of the secant variety. In algebraic geometry, the degree of a projective variety of codimension $c$ is the number of intersection points it has with a general linear subspace of dimension $c$. Since $X$ has a codimension of 2 in $\mathbb{P}^{31}$, its degree is the number of points in the intersection $X \cap \mathbb{P}^2$ for a generic plane $\mathbb{P}^2$.

Determining this degree symbolically was beyond the capabilities of existing Gröbner basis algorithms due to the extreme complexity of the 32 variables. Therefore, Oeding and Sam turned to **Numerical Algebraic Geometry (NAG)**. 

### Numerical Homotopy Continuation
NAG utilizes numerical techniques to solve systems of polynomial equations over the complex numbers. The core algorithm is numerical homotopy continuation. To find the solutions of a difficult target system $T(x) = 0$, one constructs a start system $S(x) = 0$ with known solutions, and defines a homotopy path $H(x, t) = (1-t)T(x) + \gamma t S(x) = 0$, where $t \in [cite: 1]$ and $\gamma$ is a random complex number. By tracking the known roots of $S(x)$ at $t=1$ continuously to $t=0$ using predictor-corrector methods (such as Euler prediction and Newton-Raphson correction), one can isolate the roots of the target system $T(x)$ [cite: 9, 10].

### Bertini and Lemma 3.2
Oeding and Sam utilized the highly optimized software package **Bertini**, developed by Jonathan Hauenstein and others [cite: 10], to perform this numerical intersection. They randomly generated a generic $\mathbb{P}^2$ and set up a system of equations whose solutions mapped to the preimages of the intersection points. 

Through extensive path tracking, the Bertini computations isolated exactly **96 distinct intersection points** [cite: 1]. 

In the methodology of numerical algebraic geometry, computations over floating-point numbers are rigorous for establishing lower bounds but require careful interpretation for exact equalities. Because the numerical algorithms tracked 96 distinct, non-singular paths to valid solutions, the authors could say with absolute mathematical certainty that the degree of the variety must be *at least* 96. There remains an infinitesimally small theoretical possibility that paths were missed or multiple roots were masked by numerical tolerances, hence the result is phrased probabilistically. 

This critical result is enshrined in their paper as **Lemma 3.2**: 
> $\text{deg}(X) \geq 96$

The asterisk (⋆) explicitly denotes that this lemma relies on pseudo-random numerical and probabilistic computations, meaning it holds "with high probability" and serves as powerful computational evidence rather than a strict symbolic proof [cite: 1].

## Synthesis via Bézout's Theorem

With the discovery of the equations $f_6$ and $f_{16}$ and the establishment of the numerical lower bound $\text{deg}(X) \geq 96$, Oeding and Sam possessed all the necessary components to finalize their main theorem. The synthesis of these parts relies heavily on **Bézout's Theorem**.

In projective geometry, Bézout's Theorem states that if $Y$ is a complete intersection variety defined by the transverse intersection of $k$ hypersurfaces of degrees $d_1, d_2, \dots, d_k$, then the algebraic degree of $Y$ is exactly the product of the degrees of the defining hypersurfaces:
\[ \text{deg}(Y) = d_1 \times d_2 \times \dots \times d_k \]

Let $Y = V(f_6, f_{16})$ be the variety cut out by the two equations discovered by Oeding and Sam. Because these two equations are algebraically independent and form a regular sequence, $Y$ is a complete intersection. By Bézout's theorem, the degree of $Y$ is calculated as:
\[ \text{deg}(Y) = \deg(f_6) \times \deg(f_{16}) = 6 \times 16 = 96 \]

We now have the following logical chain [cite: 1]:
1. The secant variety $X$ is contained within $Y$ because $f_6$ and $f_{16}$ vanish on $X$ ($X \subseteq Y$).
2. Both $X$ and $Y$ are irreducible varieties of codimension 2.
3. The numerical computations of Lemma 3.2 established a strict lower bound: $\text{deg}(X) \geq 96$ [cite: 1].
4. Bézout's theorem dictates that the upper-bounding variety $Y$ has exactly degree 96 [cite: 1].

Since $X$ is contained in $Y$, the degree of $X$ cannot possibly exceed the degree of $Y$. Therefore, the inequality $\text{deg}(X) \geq 96$ is forced into an exact equality: $\text{deg}(X) = 96$. Because $X \subseteq Y$, they have the same dimension, the same degree, and $Y$ is irreducible, it must be true that **$X = Y$** [cite: 1].

This elegant geometric argument decisively proves that the fifth secant variety of the Segre product of five projective lines is minimally generated by exactly two equations of degrees 6 and 16, forming a complete intersection [cite: 1].

## The Arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay Property

The discovery that $X$ is a complete intersection has profound implications for its commutative algebraic structure. In commutative algebra, a local ring is called Cohen-Macaulay if its depth is equal to its Krull dimension. For a projective variety, it is said to be **arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay (aCM)** if its homogeneous coordinate ring is a Cohen-Macaulay ring [cite: 9].

Complete intersections are the most well-behaved class of varieties in algebraic geometry, and every complete intersection is inherently arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay. Because Oeding and Sam proved that $X = V(f_6, f_{16})$ is a complete intersection, it immediately follows that $X$ is an aCM variety [cite: 1, 2].

This finding is highly significant within the broader context of algebraic geometry. There is a standing conjecture in the field that the homogeneous coordinate ring of *any* secant variety of *any* Segre product of projective spaces is arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay [cite: 1, 9, 11]. 

While the aCM property has been proven for several specific cases—such as Kanev's proof for secant varieties of Veronese varieties, and Michalek, Oeding, and Zwiernik's proof for $\sigma_2$ of Segre products using normal toric varieties [cite: 11]—the general conjecture remains open. 

When a variety is known to be aCM, it heavily constrains the length and structure of its minimal free resolution. The Betti numbers and the Cohen-Macaulay type (the rank of the last free module in the minimal free resolution) become tightly structured [cite: 9]. If an aCM scheme has a Cohen-Macaulay type of 1, it is further classified as arithmetically Gorenstein (aG) [cite: 9]. Oeding and Sam's result provides a critical, highly non-trivial data point confirming the aCM conjecture for a boundary case involving five tensor factors [cite: 2, 11].

## Generalizations: Flattening, Inheritance, and Delta-Modules

A powerful aspect of the Oeding-Sam result is that finding the defining equations for the boundary case of binary factors allows for the automatic generation of equations for much larger, more complex tensor spaces. This is achieved through the algebraic mechanisms of **inheritance** and **flattening** [cite: 1, 5].

Suppose we wish to find the equations for the fifth secant variety of a Segre product involving vector spaces of larger dimensions, such as $V_1 \otimes V_2 \dots \otimes V_5$ where $\dim(V_i) > 2$. By the principle of inheritance (introduced by Landsberg and Manivel), any tensor of rank 5 in this larger space can be projected into a $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2$ subspace [cite: 1]. Therefore, the generators $f_6$ and $f_{16}$ naturally lift to provide the minimal generators $F_6$ and $F_{16}$ for the larger tensor spaces [cite: 5].

Similarly, if we increase the number of factors (e.g., $n > 5$), we can use flattening to group the factors together until we are back to a 5-factor product, applying the known equations to those groupings [cite: 5].

### Twisted Commutative Algebras and $\Delta$-Modules
These concepts of inheritance and flattening can be rigorously formalized using the theory of **$\Delta$-modules**, developed by Andrew Snowden and Steven V. Sam. A $\Delta$-module is an algebraic structure that allows one to describe sets of equations that are invariant under symmetric group actions across varying dimensions and variables [cite: 1, 5].

The fifth secant variety of the Segre product of projective spaces is classified as a $\Delta$-variety [cite: 1, 5]. This implies that the assignment of a tuple of vector spaces to the space of degree $d$ equations vanishing on the fifth secant variety forms a finitely generated $\Delta$-module [cite: 1, 5]. Sam and Snowden previously proved a foundational theorem stating that all finitely generated $\Delta$-modules are finitely presented [cite: 1, 5]. 

Using this framework, the equations discovered by Oeding and Sam provide the exact minimal generators for these $\Delta$-modules. For $d=6$, the $\Delta$-module of equations is minimally generated by five copies of the Schur functor $S_{3,3}$ (denoted $S_{3,3} \boxtimes \dots \boxtimes S_{3,3}$) [cite: 1, 5]. For $d=16$, it is generated by five copies of $S_{8,8}$ [cite: 5]. This elegant representation-theoretic packaging means that Oeding and Sam have effectively solved the degree 6 and degree 16 defining equations for the 5th secant variety of *any* Segre product of projective spaces, regardless of the dimension or the number of factors.

## Other Lower Bounds in the Theory of Secant Varieties

While Lemma 3.2 by Oeding and Sam provides a numerical lower bound for the *algebraic degree* of a specific secant variety, the phrase "lower bounds" in the context of secant varieties often refers to bounds on the *dimension* of the variety or bounds on the *border rank* of specific tensors.

### Zak's Theorem on Linear Normality
Classically, one of the most famous lower bounds regarding secant varieties is Zak's Theorem on linear normality. F. L. Zak established sharp lower bounds for the dimension of higher secant varieties under suitable regularity assumptions on the base variety $X$ [cite: 12, 13]. Zak's work utilized theorems on tangencies, defining the concept of $J_k$-tangency to prove that if a variety is sufficiently well-behaved, its secant varieties must grow in dimension at a predictable rate until they fill the ambient space [cite: 12]. Varieties that exactly attain Zak's lower bounds are heavily constrained and are classified as Severi varieties [cite: 12, 13].

### Lower Bounds for Border Rank
In modern complexity theory, establishing lower bounds for the border rank of tensors is a holy grail. If $T$ is a tensor representing a computational task (like matrix multiplication), its border rank dictates the asymptotic computational complexity of that task.

Secant varieties directly provide these lower bounds. Because $\sigma_r(X)$ contains all tensors of border rank $\leq r$, if we can find a polynomial $P \in I(\sigma_r(X))$ such that $P(T) \neq 0$, we have unequivocally proven that the border rank of $T$ is strictly greater than $r$. 

Oeding has contributed significantly to this area by developing **Young flattenings** and **Koszul flattenings** [cite: 3, 14]. Young flattenings are equivariant linear maps between Schur modules that yield determinantal equations for secant varieties [cite: 15]. These flattenings are specifically designed to certify border-rank lower bounds [cite: 15]. In cases where classical flattenings fail (such as the matrix multiplication tensor or specific monomials), carefully chosen Young flattenings can provide optimal lower bounds [cite: 3]. For example, Oeding introduced "partial Young flattenings" to establish lower bounds on the border rank of monomials, yielding results that perfectly match upper bounds derived by Landsberg and Teitler [cite: 3, 14].

Furthermore, researchers have investigated the multiplicativity of border rank lower bounds under tensor products. Christandl, Jensen, and Zuiddam demonstrated that while border rank is generally submultiplicative, flattening-based lower bounds can exhibit multiplicativity, allowing for non-trivial lower bounds on asymptotic tensor rank [cite: 8]. 

## Applications in Complexity Theory and Quantum Information

The theoretical machinery developed by Oeding and Sam to compute defining equations for secant varieties is actively deployed in multiple applied domains.

### Geometric Complexity Theory
In Geometric Complexity Theory (GCT), a program initiated by Mulmuley and Sohoni, the goal is to separate complexity classes (like P vs NP) by comparing orbit closures of specific polynomials [cite: 4, 6]. This involves evaluating whether a "hard" polynomial (like the permanent) lies within the secant variety or orbit closure of an "easy" polynomial (like the determinant). The lower bounds achieved via metapolynomials and highest weight vectors are intimately related to the equations of secant varieties [cite: 4]. The techniques used to find $f_6$ and $f_{16}$—specifically utilizing highest weight vectors of representations of Lie groups and evaluating them on tensors—are precisely the mathematical tools needed to push GCT forward [cite: 4].

### Quantum Entanglement
In quantum information theory, the state of a multi-particle system is represented by a tensor in a Hilbert space $H^{\otimes m}$ [cite: 16]. A state is considered unentangled (or separable) if it can be written as a pure tensor product $\phi_1 \otimes \dots \otimes \phi_m$. Geometrically, the set of all unentangled states forms the Segre variety [cite: 16].

Highly entangled states correspond to tensors of high rank. To classify the type of entanglement across multiple qubits, physicists use invariants under Stochastic Local Operations with Classical Communication (SLOCC) [cite: 17]. These SLOCC invariants are algebraically identical to the defining equations of the secant varieties of the Segre product [cite: 17].

For bipartite systems (2 particles), entanglement is easily measured by the determinant of the matrix flattening. However, for multi-particle systems, higher-degree invariants are required [cite: 16]. The degree 6 and 16 equations discovered by Oeding and Sam act as precise algebraic observables for testing specific entanglement classifications in 5-qubit systems. If a 5-qubit state evaluates to a non-zero value under the $f_{16}$ polynomial, it serves as a rigorous proof that the quantum state possesses a high degree of multiparty entanglement that cannot be approximated by a sum of 5 separable states [cite: 16].
