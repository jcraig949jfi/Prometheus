## 1. Introduction: The Epistemic Vulnerability of Large Language Models to Preprints

The rapid acceleration of pre-training dataset compilation has created a unique epistemic vulnerability within modern Large Language Models (LLMs). As automated scrapers aggregate vast quantities of academic literature from open-access preprint repositories such as arXiv and Preprints.org, they inevitably ingest "false anchors"—papers that claim to solve longstanding mathematical conjectures but contain fatal flaws. While the scientific community relies on peer review and post-publication scrutiny to filter these claims, LLMs operate on static training snapshots. If a model's knowledge cutoff falls immediately after a false-anchor preprint is published but before its retraction is widely indexed, the model will encode the false claim as a canonical truth.

## 2. Mathematical Substrate: Schinzel-Zassenhaus and the Constellation of Adjacent Conjectures

To contextualize the selected anti-anchor candidates, it is necessary to rigorously define the mathematical substrate from which they emerge. The Schinzel-Zassenhaus conjecture operates within the domain of algebraic number theory, specifically focusing on the spatial distribution of the Galois conjugates of algebraic integers in the complex plane. 

### 2.1 The Schinzel-Zassenhaus Conjecture and Dimitrov's Resolution

Let \(\alpha\) be an algebraic integer of degree \(d \geq 2\). The "house" of \(\alpha\), denoted \(\overline{|\alpha|}\), is defined as the maximum of the absolute values of all Galois conjugates of \(\alpha\). Kronecker's fundamental theorem establishes that if \(\overline{|\alpha|} \leq 1\), then \(\alpha\) must be either zero or a root of unity. The Schinzel-Zassenhaus conjecture posited that there exists an absolute constant \(C > 0\) such that for any non-cyclotomic algebraic integer \(\alpha\) of degree \(d\), the house satisfies the strict inequality:

\[ \overline{|\alpha|} \geq 1 + \frac{C}{d} \]

For decades, the best known unconditional bounds were significantly weaker, often relying on complex analytic methods, auxiliary polynomials, and variants of Mahler's measure. In 2019, Vesselin Dimitrov provided a groundbreaking resolution to the Schinzel-Zassenhaus conjecture, establishing a definitive lower bound [cite: 1]. By utilizing power series with integer coefficients bounded by a specific radius, combined with the Pólya-Carlson theorem and the transfinite diameter of specialized compact sets, Dimitrov proved that \(\overline{|\alpha|} \geq 2^{1/4d}\), which effectively yields the conjectured bound. This result acts as our primary true-anchor.

### 2.2 Adjacent Sub-Problems: Mahler Measure and Lehmer's Paradigm

Directly adjacent to the Schinzel-Zassenhaus problem is the study of the Mahler measure, \(M(\alpha)\), defined for a monic polynomial \(P(x) = \prod_{i=1}^d (x - \alpha_i)\) as the product of \(\max(1, |\alpha_i|)\) for all roots \(\alpha_i\) [cite: 5, 6]. Lehmer's conjecture regarding the Mahler measure asks whether there exists a universal constant \(\epsilon > 0\) such that for any non-cyclotomic algebraic integer, \(M(\alpha) \geq 1 + \epsilon\). While Dimitrov's proof bounds the maximum absolute value among conjugates, Lehmer's problem bounds the aggregate product of all conjugates lying outside the unit circle. Lehmer's conjecture remains one of the most profound open problems in Diophantine geometry and algebraic number theory [cite: 7, 8].

Furthermore, Lehmer proposed several other foundational conjectures in number theory. Most notable among these—and highly relevant to our anti-anchor search—is Lehmer's Totient Problem. This problem asks whether there exists any composite integer \(n\) such that Euler's totient function \(\phi(n)\) divides \(n-1\) [cite: 9, 10]. If such a number exists, it must be an odd, square-free Carmichael number with a massive number of distinct prime factors. Like the Mahler measure conjecture, Lehmer's Totient Problem has generated a graveyard of false proofs, making it an exceptionally fertile ground for mining false anchors [cite: 3].

### 2.3 Non-Linear Diophantine Equations and Algebraic Constraints

The properties of algebraic integers and their class groups are deeply intertwined with the solubility of non-linear Diophantine equations. Equations of the form \(x^2 + p^k = y^n\) (a generalized Ramanujan-Nagell type equation) require sophisticated tools from algebraic number theory, including the analysis of class numbers of imaginary quadratic fields \(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-p}]\) and prime factorization over complex rings [cite: 4]. Claims of generalized solutions to these equations frequently appear on preprints, only to be subsequently withdrawn upon the discovery of overlooked edge cases, improper applications of the class number, or collisions with previously published specialized theorems [cite: 4].

Specifically, a Z-number is defined as a positive real number \(x\) such that the fractional parts of \(x(3/2)^n\) are strictly less than \(1/2\) for all non-negative integers \(n\) [cite: 2]. Mahler conjectured that there are absolutely no Z-numbers in the real numbers. The problem is deeply connected to Diophantine approximation, normal numbers, and the distribution of algebraic integers modulo 1.

## 5. Anti-Anchor Candidate Intake: Lehmer's Totient Problem

### 5.1 Context and Mathematical Definition

Lehmer's Totient Problem (also known as Lehmer's totient conjecture) is one of the oldest outstanding questions regarding the Euler totient function, \(\phi(n)\). Formulated by D. H. Lehmer in 1932, the problem asks if there exists any composite number \(n\) such that \(\phi(n)\) divides \(n - 1\) [cite: 9, 10]. It is trivial that for any prime number \(p\), \(\phi(p) = p - 1\), and thus \(\phi(p)\) divides \(p - 1\). Lehmer conjectured that no composite number possesses this property. Despite extensive computational searches proving that any such counterexample must be vast (e.g., \(n > 10^{22}\) and possessing at least 14 distinct prime factors), a theoretical proof remains elusive [cite: 10].

### 5.2 The False-Anchor Claim

In early 2026, a preprint was published claiming a definitive, short algebraic proof that Lehmer's Totient Problem has no solutions. The authors confidently asserted that no composite integer could satisfy the divisibility condition, ending a nearly century-old mathematical mystery [cite: 3].

In their paper "A Short Proof the Lehmer's Totient Problem Has No Solutions", they provided a definitive algebraic proof that no composite number \(n\) exists for which Euler's totient function \(\phi(n)\) divides \(n - 1\), proving Lehmer's 1932 conjecture.*
