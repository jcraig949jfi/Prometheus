## 2. Instrument-Disagreement Methodologies in ML/Science (2024–2026)

The concept of "instrument disagreement" in the context of Lethe and Stygian is a specialized instantiation of a broader machine learning challenge: quantifying epistemic uncertainty through ensemble or multi-modal disagreement. Between 2024 and 2026, the scientific community has extensively studied how the disagreement between predictive models (or between an LLM and a symbolic solver) can be harnessed, measured, and interpreted. 

Three primary domains of instrument-disagreement methodologies have emerged in recent literature, offering direct analogs to the Lethe-vs-Stygian architecture.

### 2.1 Deep Ensembles for ENSO Uncertainty Quantification

In high-stakes climate prediction, distinguishing between aleatoric uncertainty (inherent system noise) and epistemic uncertainty (model ignorance) is paramount. A seminal 2025/2026 study by McAfee and Barnes leverages deep ensembles—ensembles of probabilistic neural networks—to predict the El-Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) in the Community Earth System Model 2 Large Ensemble (CESM2-LE) [cite: 1]. 

The methodology treats individual neural network members as separate "instruments." The core finding of McAfee and Barnes is that epistemic uncertainty, explicitly modeled by **ensemble disagreement**, robustly signals predictive error growth associated with climate-change-induced distributional shifts [cite: 2, 3]. As the underlying distributions of sea-surface temperature and ocean heat content shift under climate change scenarios, the individual models within the ensemble begin to disagree wildly [cite: 1, 2]. 

Crucially, McAfee and Barnes demonstrated that while aleatoric uncertainty behaves counterintuitively under out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts, ensemble disagreement (epistemic uncertainty) serves as a faithful, coherent signal of model confusion [cite: 1, 3]. 

**Application to G20:** The McAfee-Barnes methodology provides a mathematical foundation for treating the disagreement between Lethe and Stygian not merely as an "error," but as a quantifiable signal of OOD mathematical conjecture space. When Lethe and Stygian disagree, it indicates that the conjecture lies in an epistemic blind spot for the stochastic model.

### 2.2 Ensemble Disagreement in Machine-Learned Interatomic Potentials (MLIPs)

While climate modeling shows the strengths of disagreement metrics, research in physical chemistry has exposed its critical vulnerabilities. Machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) are neural networks designed to bypass Newtonian equations and predict per-atom energies directly [cite: 4, 5]. 

In 2025 and 2026, Kurniawan et al. systematically assessed the relationship between predictive precision and true accuracy in MLIPs using ensemble-based uncertainty quantification methods (e.g., bootstrap, dropout, snapshot ensembles) [cite: 6, 7]. Their findings serve as a stark warning: in OOD regimes, ensemble disagreement often correlates poorly with true per-molecule prediction error [cite: 5, 8]. 

Kurniawan et al. showed that ensemble uncertainty estimates can behave counterintuitively, often "plateauing or even decreasing as predictive errors grow" [cite: 4, 7]. Because deep learning models can become uniformly overconfident in highly unfamiliar configuration spaces, ensemble disagreement vanishes, giving a false sense of reliability [cite: 6, 8]. This has led researchers to pivot toward post-hoc, learned discriminative classifiers (such as PROBE) acting on frozen per-atom representations, recasting uncertainty quantification as selective classification rather than error regression [cite: 4, 8].

**Application to G20:** The Kurniawan et al. findings perfectly encapsulate the **Vacuous-until-Lethe-v2** problem. Just as MLIP ensembles experience "epistemic uncertainty collapse" and stop disagreeing in complex OOD spaces [cite: 5, 9], modern Lethe models have collapsed into a homogenized, overly cautious predictive distribution. Lethe fails to clash with Stygian because its internal parameters prevent it from venturing into the raw, confident errors required to trigger an `instrument_clash_detected` event.

### 2.3 Hybrid LLM-Symbolic Solver (LLM-SS) Disagreements

The most direct analog to the Lethe (LLM) and Stygian (Code Battery) dynamic is the emerging paradigm of LLM-Symbolic Solvers (LLM-SS) [cite: 10]. In these architectures, the LLM acts as a semantic translator and heuristic generator, while a downstream symbolic solver (e.g., Z3, Prover9, SymPy) performs verifiable computation [cite: 10, 11].

Recent 2024-2025 literature highlights the inherent "instrument disagreement" embedded in these hybrid systems. LLMs encode information in continuous vector representations, making them highly susceptible to logical inconsistency and hallucination under slight prompt perturbations [cite: 12]. In contrast, symbolic systems are strictly deterministic [cite: 12, 13]. 

When evaluating LLM-generated equation hypotheses, automated filtering frameworks rely on symbolic solvers to detect disagreements. For instance, in scientific equation discovery, SymPy's symbolic solver is used as an instrument to check the analytical solvability of LLM outputs, routinely eliminating significant percentages (e.g., 11.3%) of candidates that represent transcendental or intractable equations disguised as plausible LLM output [cite: 14]. Furthermore, complex adversarial interactions are now intentionally designed between LLMs and logic programs to map multi-step reasoning failures, where the symbolic solver forces the LLM to recursively withdraw tentative conclusions upon the presentation of strict logical contradictions [cite: 11, 13].

**Application to G20:** The LLM-SS research confirms that LLMs are structurally incapable of replacing deterministic symbolic engines. The disagreement between continuous linguistic generalization (Lethe) and discrete rule application (Stygian) is a fundamental feature of hybrid neuro-symbolic systems, necessitating deliberate extraction frameworks like G20 to monitor the boundary where language approximation fails mathematical rigor.

Lethe, in its current state, rarely provides confident, mathematically unsound verdicts that a deterministic code battery (Stygian) can overtly refute. To awaken the G20 diagnostic engine prior to the arrival of Lethe v2, artificial environments must be constructed to seed instrument disagreement.

### 3.1 Synthetic Test Bed: Small-LLM vs. Deterministic Batteries

The most immediate solution to the vacuousness problem is bypassing the primary, heavily aligned Lethe model in favor of a smaller, more volatile LLM. 

**Proposal:** Construct a distinct loader loop that utilizes a highly quantized or inherently weaker frontier model (e.g., `gpt-4o-mini`, Llama-3-8B) functioning as a "Pseudo-Lethe." This model is deliberately prompted with complex, esoteric number theory conjectures (e.g., properties of Carmichael numbers, intricate Diophantine equations) where linguistic plausibility frequently diverges from mathematical reality.

This Pseudo-Lethe's output is then juxtaposed against a highly specialized, deterministic Stygian battery, such as a **Mossinghoff battery** (used for polynomial irreducibility and root bounding). Because the small LLM lacks the parameter depth to simulate the symbolic reasoning required for these specific polynomials, it will frequently hallucinate plausible-sounding but mathematically false assertions. The Stygian battery will instantly falsify these assertions, reliably generating robust `instrument_clash_detected` events, thus flooding the G20 plugin with valid test data.

**Proposal:** Deploy a **Corrupted-Stygian** module. In this paradigm, a secondary code battery is engineered with a subtle, deterministic error. For example:

When Lethe (operating correctly based on standard mathematical texts) encounters this problem, it will predict the mathematically standard outcome. The Corrupted-Stygian will execute its logic and return an objectively incorrect result. G20 will monitor this interaction, completely agnostic to *which* instrument is fundamentally correct, and flag the `instrument_clash_detected`. ## 4. Concept-K Extraction: Transitioning to Deep Semantic MNER

In G20 v1, "Concept K" (the specific mathematical topic causing the instrument disagreement) is extracted using a highly naive heuristic: extracting shared tokens of \(\geq 6\) characters between the Lethe and Stygian outputs and selecting the alphabetically first token. This methodology is fundamentally unscientific and highly susceptible to noise (e.g., selecting words like "Theorem," "Assume," or "Equation" instead of the actual mathematical concept like "Homeomorphism" or "Eigenvalue").

To achieve substrate-grade reliability, v2 must abandon string-matching in favor of state-of-the-art NLP models designed explicitly for mathematics, incorporating BERT-style semantic similarity, tf-idf weighting, and Mathematical Named Entity Recognition (MNER).

### 4.1 BERT-Style Semantic Similarity and MathBERT

String-based metrics (like BLEU or naive token overlap) rely on surface-level n-gram overlap, which makes them sensitive to inconsequential lexical variations while ignoring the underlying semantic content [cite: 15]. In mathematical discourse, two instruments might describe the exact same concept using entirely different vocabularies (e.g., "roots of the polynomial" vs. "zeros of the algebraic equation").

**MathBERT** [cite: 16, 17] provides the solution. By integrating Abstract Syntax Trees (ASTs) and Operator Tree (OPT) structures during pretraining, MathBERT enhances semantic understanding of mathematical terminology, equations, and problem statements that general language models struggle to identify accurately [cite: 15, 16]. 

For Concept K extraction, the outputs of Lethe and Stygian must be mapped into continuous vector spaces (embeddings) using a model like MathBERT or Llemma [cite: 18]. The embeddings of the respective outputs can then be compared using cosine similarity to isolate the specific clauses or sentences that represent the core mathematical disagreement, filtering out generic syntactic boilerplate [cite: 15, 16].

### 4.2 Mathematical Named Entity Recognition (MNER)

The most critical upgrade for G20 v2 is the implementation of MNER. Named Entity Recognition is the process of locating and classifying entities in unstructured text into predefined categories [cite: 19, 20, 21]. In the mathematical domain, MNER locates concepts, formulas, variables, and theorems [cite: 22].

Recent literature (2024-2025) provides two highly sophisticated architectures capable of serving as the backend for Concept K extraction.

#### 4.2.1 The LERT-BiGRU-IDCNN-CRF Architecture (Zheng et al., 2025)
Zheng et al. (2025) proposed a breakthrough model for Chinese mathematical knowledge entity recognition, achieving an impressive F1 score of 97.34% [cite: 23, 24]. The architecture is composed of four distinct layers, which can be adapted for G20's Concept K extraction engine:

1.  **LERT (Linguistically Motivated Pre-trained Model):** Provides context-related word vectors by capturing linguistic features alongside a Masked Language Model (MLM) task [cite: 23, 24]. LERT overcomes the limitations of standard BERT by deeply embedding syntactic dependencies.
2.  **BiGRU (Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit):** Processes the sequence to capture both long-distance and short-distance contextual information within the Lethe/Stygian outputs [cite: 23].
3.  **IDCNN (Iterated Dilated Convolutional Neural Network):** Retrieves local information and captures entity boundaries without the performance bottleneck of traditional sequential processing [cite: 23].
4.  **CRF (Conditional Random Field):** Performs the final sequence labeling. While LERT and BiGRU capture semantic meaning, the CRF decodes the entity labels by mathematically leveraging the transition dependencies between labels (e.g., ensuring a "Begin-Entity" tag is logically followed by an "Inside-Entity" tag) [cite: 23, 25].

#### 4.2.2 The AT-BSAC Architecture (Lai et al., 2024)
Alternatively, Lai et al. (2024) introduced the Adversarial Training and Bidirectional long short-term memory-Self-attention Conditional random field (AT-BSAC) model, achieving an F1 score of 93.74% [cite: 26]. 
*   This model applies small perturbations to the word vectors during training to generate adversarial samples, significantly increasing the model's robustness against the "fuzzy entity boundaries" common in complex mathematical texts [cite: 26].
*   It incorporates a self-attention mechanism to extract long-distance dependencies between mathematical entities [cite: 26].

**Implementation in G20:** By passing Lethe and Stygian output logs through an MNER model based on these 2024/2025 architectures, G20 can bypass naive string extraction. The model will natively recognize and tag entities such as `[CONCEPT: Riemannian Manifold]` or `[THEOREM: Fermat's Little Theorem]`. If both instruments contain tags representing the same mathematical space, the highest-confidence shared entity is formally extracted as **Concept K**.

### 4.3 TF-IDF Weighting for Concept K Isolation

Even with MNER, there is a risk of extracting ubiquitous mathematical entities (e.g., "Integer", "Function", "Matrix") that do not represent the *specific* locus of disagreement. 

To resolve this, the MNER pipeline must be augmented with Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) weighting. 
*   **Term Frequency (TF):** Measures how frequently the extracted entity appears in the localized instrument logs.
*   **Inverse Document Frequency (IDF):** The corpus for IDF calculation must be a massive database of general mathematical proofs (e.g., the Lean Mathlib or a corpus of arXiv mathematics papers). 

By applying TF-IDF weighting to the entities extracted by the LERT-BiGRU-IDCNN-CRF module, common mathematical "stop-words" (like "Equation" or "Set") will be severely penalized due to their high frequency across the global corpus. Rare, highly specific entities (e.g., "Kähler manifold", "Dedekind zeta function") will receive massive weight boosts. The highest-weighted shared entity is designated as the definitive Concept K.

Pollux evaluates a conjecture and outputs a boolean verdict with a proof trace. Stygian evaluates the same conjecture. If `Pollux.verdict != Stygian.verdict`, the `instrument_clash_detected` trigger is pulled, capturing the raw logs of both systems.

### 5.2 Step 2: Semantic Concept K Extraction Pipeline

Upon detecting the clash, the raw logs are routed to the NLP pipeline.

1.  **Embedding:** Logs are tokenized and embedded using MathBERT [cite: 16, 17].
2.  **MNER Tagging:** The LERT-BiGRU-IDCNN-CRF model scans the text, extracting all valid mathematical named entities [cite: 23].
3.  **TF-IDF Filtering:** The extracted entities are weighted against a mathematical corpus.
4.  **Concept K Selection:** The entity with the highest TF-IDF score present in the local context window of the clash is locked in as Concept K.

### 5.3 Step 3: The Ablation-Test Loader Protocol

The true diagnostic power of G20 v2 lies in determining *why* the clash occurred. This requires an ablation-test loop.

Once Concept K (e.g., "Euler characteristic") is identified, the loader automatically modifies the original prompt sent to the LLM/Heuristic instrument. It systematically masks or excises all references to Concept K.

```python
def execute_ablation_test(original_prompt, concept_k, llm_model):
    # Strip Concept K from the prompt
    ablated_prompt = remove_entity(original_prompt, concept_k)
    
    # Re-prompt the instrument
    new_verdict, new_log = llm_model.evaluate(ablated_prompt)
    
    return new_verdict
```

If the LLM's verdict shifts from `True` to `False` (or vice versa) simply because the specific noun representing Concept K was removed, the system has proven that the instrument disagreement is purely semantic/lexical, rather than fundamentally mathematical.

### 5.4 Step 4: Novel Kill Patterns

Based on the execution of the ablation test and the NLP pipeline, G20 v2 will emit one of three distinct kill patterns to the broader substrate:

1.  `instrument_clash_detected`: The baseline emission indicating a genuine, unresolved mathematical conflict between the two instruments.
2.  `concept_K_extraction_failed`: Emitted if the LERT-BiGRU-IDCNN-CRF module fails to find any mathematically significant entity with a TF-IDF weight above a predefined threshold. This indicates the disagreement is likely syntactical garbage or formatting errors, rather than a mathematical dispute.
3.  `instrument_disagreement_explained_by_context`: Emitted if the Ablation-Test Loader reveals that stripping Concept K alters the LLM's verdict. This proves that the LLM was hallucinating a localized association with the specific named entity, rather than engaging in robust logical deduction.

## 6. Meta-Epistemic Finding Framing

The outputs generated by G20—specifically `instrument_disagreement_explained_by_context`—are fundamentally distinct from the outputs of primary mathematical solvers. G20 does not prove that a polynomial has roots, nor does it establish bounds on a prime gap. 

G20 generates **meta-epistemic claims**. It outputs data regarding the boundaries, internal logic, and failure modes of the AI's own reasoning substrate. It maps *how the machine knows*, rather than *what is mathematically true*. 

### 6.1 The Quarantine Protocol: Substrate Consumption

Because meta-epistemic claims are not mathematical truths, they pose a severe contamination risk if fed back into the primary mathematical substrate. If a formal proof database ingests a log detailing how Lethe hallucinated a false property of the Riemann zeta function, future LLM training runs might ingest that hallucination as a legitimate mathematical discussion.

**Proposal:** G20 findings must be strictly quarantined into a **Separate Ledger**, isolated entirely from the primary mathematical knowledge graph. 

### 6.2 Routing to Aporia Self-Audit

The ideal destination for these meta-epistemic findings is the **Aporia self-audit system**. Aporia acts as the introspective, system-health monitor of the AI architecture. 

When G20 identifies a specific Concept K (e.g., "Kähler geometry") that consistently triggers instrument clashes and ablation-test verdict shifts, it routes this data to Aporia. Aporia then updates the system's global "Confidence Heatmap." The next time Lethe attempts to generate a conjecture involving Kähler geometry, Aporia dynamically lowers Lethe's confidence weighting and automatically demands higher validation thresholds from Stygian, knowing that Lethe possesses a demonstrated epistemic blind spot in this exact semantic domain.

### 6.3 Triaging by Frontier Reviewers

In addition to automated routing, high-frequency G20 kill patterns should be surfaced to human frontier reviewers. These reviewers do not check the math—Stygian has already done that. Instead, human reviewers analyze the *nature* of the epistemic collapse to adjust RLHF reward models or alter pre-training dataset compositions for Lethe v2, physically removing the contaminated texts that caused Lethe to confidently hallucinate in the face of Stygian's deterministic reality.

## 7. The Contrarian Steelman: G20 as an Epistemic Distraction

While the engineering and NLP architectures proposed above are highly sophisticated, a rigorous scientific argument can be constructed against the very existence of the G20 plugin. From the perspective of pure mathematics and substrate optimization, one can forcefully argue that **G20 is a fundamental distraction and should be deprecated.**

### 7.1 The Primacy of Substrate-Grade Mathematics

The ultimate goal of automated theorem proving and mathematical AI is to expand the boundaries of human knowledge by discovering, verifying, and formalizing mathematical truths. Systems like Lean, Coq, and the deterministic Stygian battery deal in objective, substrate-grade reality. A proof is either valid, or it is not. 

In this pursuit, the internal psychological or "epistemic" state of a Large Language Model is scientifically uninteresting. An LLM's hallucination regarding a Diophantine equation is not a new class of mathematical object; it is simply a probabilistic calculation error caused by continuous-vector space approximation [cite: 12, 13].

### 7.2 The Hallucination Exhaustion Argument

Advocates for deprecating G20 argue that mapping the exact semantic reasons *why* an LLM hallucinates (using complex tools like MNER, TF-IDF, and ablation testing) is equivalent to studying the static on a broken television set. 

LLMs lack true logical reasoning ability; they synthesize language based on training distributions [cite: 11]. When an LLM (Lethe) disagrees with a deterministic solver (Stygian), the reason is always, trivially, the same: the LLM is a stochastic pattern-matcher operating outside its interpolation bounds. Spending computational resources and engineering hours to pinpoint that "Concept K = Eigenvalue" triggered the hallucination provides zero utility to the actual resolution of the math problem. 

As noted in the interatomic potential research, when neural networks operate out-of-distribution, their uncertainty metrics and disagreements become unreliable and arbitrary [cite: 4, 6, 8]. Attempting to derive deep epistemic meaning from random continuous-vector misalignments is a category error.

### 7.3 Deprecation and Resource Reallocation

The contrarian position dictates that the entire G20 diagnostic layer should be deprecated. If Lethe and Stygian disagree, the architecture should natively default to Stygian's deterministic verdict—end of protocol. The LLM's output should be discarded as a failed heuristic search branch.

By deprecating G20, massive computational resources currently allocated to running MathBERT embeddings, LERT-BiGRU-IDCNN-CRF models [cite: 23], and continuous ablation-test re-prompting loops can be reallocated to the Stygian battery. Instead of analyzing *why* an LLM failed, those compute cycles could be used to push the deterministic solver one node deeper into the formal proof search tree, accelerating the actual discovery of substrate-grade mathematical claims.
