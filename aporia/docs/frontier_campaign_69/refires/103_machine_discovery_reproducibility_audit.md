# Reproducibility Audit of AM and EURISKO

Key points:
- The Automated Mathematician (AM) and EURISKO systems are strictly irreproducible due to the absence of a rigorous, quantifiable evaluation baseline in their original publications [cite: 1, 2].
- The original Lisp source code, long thought lost or deliberately hidden, was recovered in 2023 from the Stanford AI Laboratory archives following Douglas Lenat's death [cite: 3, 4].
- Extensive methodological critiques, notably by Ritchie and Hanna (1984), demonstrated that the system's discoveries relied heavily on human interpretation, implicit hand-holding, and the innate structure of the Lisp programming language rather than autonomous intelligence [cite: 5, 6].
- Because the evaluations relied on Lenat's subjective judgment of interestingness and interactive guidance rather than standardized datasets or automated rules, no faithful reproduction can be verified against a published metric [cite: 7, 8].

These systems represent a fascinating era of symbolic artificial intelligence, characterized by profound ambition and significant methodological informality. Douglas Lenat's PhD thesis system, AM, aimed to simulate mathematical discovery by applying heuristic rules to basic set theory concepts, eventually claiming to rediscover arithmetic, primes, and Goldbach's conjecture [cite: 9, 10]. Its successor, EURISKO, extended this concept to discover heuristics about heuristics, achieving fame by winning the Traveller Trillion Credit Squadron national championships [cite: 11, 12]. However, the legacy of these systems is deeply intertwined with controversy regarding their reproducibility and the true autonomy of their discoveries. This report provides an exhaustive reproducibility audit of AM and EURISKO, detailing the surviving artifacts, the nature of the published claims, the dependencies that prevent true reproduction, the standing critiques that re-evaluated the original results, and the modern systems that currently approximate this line of research.

The Automated Mathematician and EURISKO cannot be faithfully reproduced against a published result because no rigid, quantitative result or objective evaluation rule was ever published to check a reproduction against [cite: 1, 2]. PART 2. THE ARTEFACT TRAIL
For over four decades, the source code for AM and EURISKO was considered a lost artifact of the symbolic AI era. While AM was technically available to fellow researchers at Stanford in the late 1970s, Lenat became notoriously protective of his software shortly thereafter and kept the EURISKO source code strictly confidential during his lifetime [cite: 4, 9, 11]. Papers written about these systems consistently avoided technical implementation details, and Lenat frequently claimed he had not retained any copies of the original programs [cite: 4].

The artefact trail went cold until late 2023, following Douglas Lenat's death in August of that year [cite: 13, 14]. Lenat's passing caused the password protection to break on his personal directory within the Stanford AI Laboratory (SAIL) backup data, specifically the SAILDART archive maintained by Bruce Baumgart [cite: 3, 4, 15]. Within the user directory DBL (Douglas Bruce Lenat), researchers discovered the original, intact files for both AM and EURISKO, alongside the input data for the Traveller space combat RPG [cite: 3, 4]. 

The original AM source code (circa 1977) has been extracted and is now hosted on GitHub by user white-flame. It is available at the bare URL https://github.com/white-flame/am [cite: 4, 5, 16, 17]. The repository contains the original loader file LT, as well as the sub-files TOP6, CON6, and UTIL6 exactly as referenced in Lenat's 1976 PhD thesis [cite: 4, 17]. 

The original EURISKO source code (circa 1981) was also extracted from the SAILDART archive and is hosted at the bare URL https://github.com/white-flame/eurisko [cite: 4, 15, 16]. The repository notes that the entire program is contained within a file named EUR, though it lacks some of the later infrastructure described in subsequent publications [cite: 15]. A runnable port of this EURISKO code, adapted for the Medley Interlisp environment with minimal modifications by user seveno4, exists at the bare URL https://github.com/seveno4/EURISKO [cite: 4, 16, 18]. 

Several reimplementations have been attempted over the decades, though none establish true scientific reproducibility. Ken Haase, a student of Marvin Minsky at MIT, wrote a system called Cyrano as part of his 1990 PhD thesis [cite: 5, 19]. Cyrano was a rational reconstruction and epistemological analysis of AM and EURISKO [cite: 5, 19]. Haase claimed structural inspiration rather than exact execution faithfulness, aiming to build better representation languages rather than explicitly duplicate Lenat's runtime traces [cite: 5, 20]. Another major reimplementation attempt was undertaken by Bruce Porter's research group at the University of Texas at Austin [cite: 21]. They built a version of AM in the Prolog language. This Prolog reimplementation is currently archived on GitHub by user akkartik at the bare URL https://github.com/akkartik/am-utexas [cite: 21, 22, 23]. However, because Prolog's logic programming paradigm differs radically from the procedural Lisp environment that birthed AM, this project serves as a conceptual reconstruction rather than a faithful, bit-for-bit proxy [cite: 23, 24]. 

PART 3. THE PUBLISHED RESULT TO CHECK AGAINST
The foundational texts for these systems are Douglas Lenat's 1976 Stanford PhD thesis, "AM: An artificial intelligence approach to discovery in mathematics as heuristic search", and his subsequent 1983 and 1984 papers in the Artificial Intelligence journal, most notably "Why AM and EURISKO appear to work", DOI 10.1016/0004-3702(84)90016-X [cite: 5, 12, 25].

The specific claims in these publications state that AM was initialized with 115 basic set theory concepts and approximately 250 heuristic rules [cite: 3, 9]. From these primitives, the system supposedly conducted hundreds of experiments and autonomously discovered the concept of natural numbers, addition, multiplication, divisors, prime numbers, maximally divisible numbers, the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, and Goldbach's conjecture [cite: 5, 9, 10]. For EURISKO, the primary published result is that the system developed new heuristics for VLSI design and evolved mutant fleet strategies that won the United States Traveller Trillion Credit Squadron national championships in 1981 and 1982 [cite: 10, 11, 12].

However, the original papers report no precise, quantitative number a reproduction could be checked against. There is no baseline accuracy metric, no rigorous count of valid versus invalid conjectures generated per compute cycle, no exact replicate count, and no mathematical formula used to evaluate the success rate of the heuristic search [cite: 2, 8]. AM did not prove the conjectures it formulated; it merely proposed them based on empirical evidence and a subjective calculation of "interestingness" [cite: 26]. Because the original publications report no exact statistical figure or rigid evaluation threshold that a modern reproduction would have to match, the system is strictly irreproducible in the sense of an exact audit [cite: 1]. 

PART 4. WHAT WOULD BREAK A REPRODUCTION
Even with the original Lisp source code recovered, a faithful reproduction of the original experimental run is broken by several systemic dependencies and methodological flaws.

First, the original systems relied heavily on interactive human input during the run, effectively constituting undocumented hand-holding. AM's heuristic Rule 2 explicitly stated, "If the user has recently referred to X, then boost the priority of any tasks involving X" [cite: 5]. This means that the human operator was dynamically steering the system's agenda toward fruitful concepts, breaking the illusion of pure autonomy [cite: 5]. Similarly, during the development of EURISKO's Traveller fleets, Lenat admitted that he would leave the system running overnight and intervene in the morning to remove errors, cull unpromising heuristics, and enter additional ones [cite: 12, 13]. The exact sequence of these human interventions was never recorded or published.

Second, the evaluation was done entirely by the author's judgment rather than by an automated rule. Lenat functioned as an external observer who looked at randomly generated short Lisp programs and interpreted them as representing sophisticated mathematical concepts [cite: 5]. Because the evaluation metric was the author's own subjective recognition of mathematical patterns, a modern programmer cannot automatically verify whether a reimplementation has successfully "discovered" a concept.

Third, the heuristic rules were deeply intertwined with the control flow logic and relied on hand-tuned parameters never fully formalized. Published versions of AM's rules involved vague, undefined terms embedded directly into the Lisp control structure. For example, Rule 218 relied on the precondition "If two expressions are structurally similar," and Rule 129 instructed the system to "replace the value obtained by some other (very similar) value" [cite: 5]. The precise computational definitions of "structurally similar" were undocumented hacks tied to the internal memory representations of the Lisp environment [cite: 5].

Finally, the execution depended on vanished hardware and legacy languages. The thesis states AM was executed on a PDP-10 KI-10 uniprocessor with 256k core memory, using the January 1975 release of Interlisp [cite: 17]. While Medley Interlisp emulators can load the syntax today [cite: 4, 18], the original system's heuristic agenda and memory-bounded search were intricately tied to the physical memory limitations and garbage collection quirks of the 1970s PDP-10 environment [cite: 17, 27].

PART 5. THE STANDING CRITIQUE OF THE ORIGINAL RESULT
AM and EURISKO were heavily challenged after publication, leading to one of the most famous methodological re-examinations in the history of artificial intelligence. The decisive critique was published by G. D. Ritchie and F. K. Hanna in their 1984 paper "AM: a case study in AI methodology" in the Artificial Intelligence journal, DOI 10.1016/0004-3702(84)90015-8 [cite: 5, 28].

Ritchie and Hanna criticized Lenat on the grounds that the theoretical content of the AM program was not clearly stated and that its success depended largely on the representation chosen and the author's own interpretation of the output [cite: 5, 6]. They pointed out that AM did not discover concepts through deep mathematical reasoning; rather, it worked by randomly mutating short Lisp strings. Because Lisp is a functional language closely related to lambda calculus and foundational logic, small, random mutations in Lisp code naturally produced operations that mapped neatly onto human mathematical concepts [cite: 5]. Ritchie and Hanna argued that Lenat over-interpreted the output, effectively looking at randomly generated functional syntax and mapping it to profound mathematical theories by hindsight [cite: 5, 8]. Furthermore, they highlighted that much of the real discovery work was buried in unexplained, intertwined control procedures rather than autonomous, explicit heuristic data structures [cite: 5].

The challenge resolved with an extraordinary concession from the original author. In the very same 1984 volume of the Artificial Intelligence journal, Douglas Lenat and John Seely Brown published "Why AM and Eurisko appear to work", DOI 10.1016/0004-3702(84)90016-X [cite: 5, 12, 29]. Lenat directly conceded to the critique, admitting that AM ran out of steam because its success was an artifact of the Lisp programming language [cite: 5, 29]. He acknowledged that any system generating enough short Lisp programs would inevitably produce strings that an external observer could interpret as equally sophisticated mathematical concepts [cite: 5]. He concluded that AM's heuristic discovery paradigm could not easily be generalized because the "meaning" of the discoveries resided entirely in the tight coupling between Lisp syntax and the human observer's interpretation, a vulnerability he attempted to patch by having EURISKO evolve its own heuristics, which eventually exhausted itself as well [cite: 3, 5, 29]. 

PART 6. IF IT CANNOT BE REPRODUCED, WHAT IS THE NEAREST HONEST THING
Because the verdict is NOT_REPRODUCIBLE, routing to a modern, scientifically verifiable proxy is necessary. The closest live substitutes in the modern field of Machine Discovery are automated mathematical conjecturing and theory formation systems based on Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Large Language Models (LLMs) grounded in dependent type theory. 

The two nearest honest substitutes are:
1. FERMAT, an open-source reinforcement learning environment for mathematical theory formation, documented in arXiv:2511.14778 [cite: 7, 30]. FERMAT models concept discovery and theorem-proving using symbolic actions and employs evolutionary algorithms and LLMs to automatically synthesize and score the "interestingness" of mathematical objects [cite: 7].
2. Minimo (Mathematics from Intrinsic Motivation), documented in arXiv:2407.00695 [cite: 31, 32]. Minimo is an agent that learns to pose challenging problems for itself and solve them by sampling valid conjectures from a language model using constrained decoding and type-directed synthesis [cite: 26, 31].

What these modern substitutes do NOT capture about the original:
These live substitutes abandon the purely open-ended, subjective Lisp mutation that defined Lenat's systems. In AM and EURISKO, a concept was just a loose bundle of Lisp functions, and a conjecture was considered valid if it met Lenat's personal aesthetic threshold for interestingness [cite: 5, 8]. Modern systems like FERMAT and Minimo do not rely on subjective human interpretation; instead, they generate conjectures that are strictly bound by the axioms of dependent type theory and rigorously verified by formal automated theorem provers like Z3, Lean, or Coq [cite: 7, 26, 32]. Furthermore, modern proxies do not capture EURISKO's attempt at meta-circular heuristic discovery. While EURISKO explicitly tried to generate rules about how to generate rules, systems like Minimo focus on self-improving the policy and value functions of a proof search tree via hindsight relabeling, leaving the fundamental rules of inference untouched [cite: 3, 31, 32]. They replace the romantic, rule-breaking heuristic search of the 1970s with rigid, mathematically verified formal logic.
