# FRONTIER PRACTITIONER DOSSIER: Automated Scientific Discovery

You are entering a field that has fundamentally transformed over the last three years, migrating from isolated combinatorial search algorithms into a highly active, often contested intersection of genetic programming, deep learning, and agentic large language models. The tacit knowledge in this discipline is heavily guarded by empirical failures: many published claims of "autonomous discovery" quietly rely on massive human pre-processing, and many benchmark results are contaminated by data memorization. 

Your understanding of the BACON algorithm is historically accurate (cite: 3, 50). BACON did indeed use data-driven heuristics to find constant terms and derive secondary theoretical terms. However, as a frontier practitioner, you must know that BACON's specific mechanism is completely obsolete. BACON relied on the "ceteris paribus" assumption: the ability to hold all variables constant except the two being analyzed (cite: 1). In real, noisy, high-dimensional data, this is impossible. The field quickly realized that heuristic stepwise derivation fails catastrophically when introduced to observational noise (cite: 48, 50). Today, the objective remains the same—rediscovering empirical laws without knowing their form in advance—but the mechanism has entirely shifted to Symbolic Regression (SR) powered by genetic programming, neural proxies, and, most recently, logic-aware systems and Large Language Models (LLMs).

Here is the exhaustive intelligence you require to build, run, and measure frontier experiments in Automated Scientific Discovery today.

PART 1. THE FIELD IN 2026, AND ITS FRONTIER

Automated Scientific Discovery today is the fusion of two formerly distinct domains: Symbolic Regression (the mathematical search for governing equations) and Agentic AI (the orchestration of research workflows, hypothesis generation, and code execution). The core goal is no longer just fitting an equation to a static CSV file; it is the closed-loop, autonomous operation of in-silico or physical laboratories where an algorithm designs the experiment, parses the resulting high-dimensional data, proposes a physically consistent mathematical law, and writes the manuscript.

What is SETTLED: The engine for equation discovery is Genetic Programming (GP), often accelerated by deep learning. The debate over whether to use pure deep learning (like Transformers) or GP for Symbolic Regression has largely settled in favor of heavily optimized, multi-population GP (such as PySR or Operon) because GP natively enforces sparsity and allows for explicit parsimony penalties, which are critical for finding interpretable scientific laws rather than complex Taylor expansions (cite: 21, 72, 75). Furthermore, it is settled that pure, unconstrained LLMs cannot perform novel mathematical discovery reliably; they hallucinate, fail on noisy data, and primarily regurgitate memorized equations (cite: 57, 79). 

What is CONTESTED: The degree of true autonomy versus "human-in-the-loop" prompting. On one side, groups like Sakana AI claim that their autonomous agents (The AI Scientist) can run the entire lifecycle of machine learning research end-to-end, generating ideas, running experiments, and passing peer review without human intervention (cite: 6, 36). On the other side, skeptics and benchmark authors point out that these systems suffer from a "tool paradox" where they prematurely exploit suboptimal solutions, produce shallow $15 papers, and collapse entirely when exposed to minor observational noise (cite: 49, 57). Another major contest is between purely data-driven discovery and theory-driven discovery. Researchers utilizing systems like AI-Descartes argue that Symbolic Regression must be constrained by automated theorem provers and formal logic axioms to ensure discoveries do not violate known physical laws (cite: 43, 56).

What is OPEN: The automated formalization of background theory. Currently, systems that ensure physical consistency require human experts to manually encode domain axioms into formal logic (cite: 45). Bridging the gap between unstructured scientific literature and formal, machine-readable axioms without human translation is the most critical open problem. Additionally, scalable Multi-view Symbolic Regression (MvSR)—finding universal constants across heterogeneous, dynamically changing datasets—remains an unsolved frontier (cite: 72).

What was lost in the merge: The field of "Equation Discovery" has been fully absorbed by "Symbolic Regression" and "Agentic AI." In this merge, the field temporarily lost its focus on deep physical interpretability, briefly trading it for the sheer predictive accuracy of neural networks. We are only now clawing back the demand for physically meaningful equations through specialized, axiom-constrained benchmark suites.

What it runs: PySR is the dominant, canonical implementation of modern Symbolic Regression. You give it a NumPy array of inputs and a vector of targets, and it distributes a multi-population genetic programming search across all your CPU cores to return a Pareto front of equations balancing accuracy and complexity (cite: 21, 75).

PART 5. THE REPRODUCTION RECIPE

The single most reproducible and informative experiment to run as a newcomer is the recovery of Kepler's Third Law or Coulomb's Law (Equation 10 in the Feynman dataset) using PySR with injected noise. This demonstrates the exact mechanics of modern GP and the critical balance of the parsimony penalty.

Software and Version:
Python 3.10+, PySR v0.19.0 (or latest stable). Julia 1.10+.

Dataset / Generator:
Use the FSReD generator or download the precomputed Feynman Equation 10 (Coulomb's Law). 
Input variables: q1, q2, r, epsilon. 
Target: Force. 
Generate 10000 rows. Inject 1 percent Gaussian noise to the Target variable.

Parameters to Set in PySR:
- binary_operators: ["+", "-", "*", "/"]
- unary_operators: ["exp", "sin"] (Include these even though Coulomb's law doesn't need them, to force the algorithm to search a larger space).
- niterations: 100
- populations: 15
- parsimony_coef: 0.001
- maxsize: 20

Seeding and Replicates:
Run 10 independent replicates using distinct random seeds (e.g., seeds 42 to 51). 

Compute Cost:
Extremely light. Approximately 0.5 CPU hours total on a modern multi-core workstation. No GPU required.

Expected Result:
PySR will return a Pandas DataFrame containing the Pareto front of equations. The equation with the lowest loss that also experiences a sudden drop in complexity (the "elbow" of the Pareto front) should perfectly match Coulomb's Law: F = (q1 * q2) / (4 * pi * epsilon * r^2). The residual variation (Mean Squared Error) should approximate the variance of the 1 percent Gaussian noise you injected. Compare against the 100 percent success rate cited in the original AI Feynman paper (cite: 17).

Three Most Common Ways People Get This Wrong:
1. Dimensionality Leakage: Users often feed the algorithm only the exact variables required for the law. In the real world, you have hundreds of irrelevant variables. If you do not pad the input dataset with dummy variables (e.g., completely random columns), you are running a toy problem, not an experiment (cite: 16).
2. Mis-tuning the Parsimony Coefficient: If you set the parsimony penalty too low, the algorithm will attempt to fit the 1 percent Gaussian noise using massive, over-parameterized Taylor expansions (a massive tree of additions and multiplications) rather than finding the simple underlying law.
3. Ignoring Julia Pre-compilation: Beginners often place the PySR call inside a fast loop without realizing the JIT compiler is re-evaluating the types, leading to catastrophic execution times.

PART 6. WHAT DOES NOT EXIST AND WOULD HAVE TO BE BUILT

If you want to operate at the frontier in 2026, you cannot rely entirely on off-the-shelf tools. The primary gap in the field is the bridge between raw, unstructured observational modalities and formal symbolic reasoning.

1. The Automated Axiom Extractor for AI-Descartes
What goes in: A corpus of scientific PDF papers or raw textual domain knowledge.
What comes out: Formal, computer-readable logical axioms (e.g., First-Order Logic constraints) suitable for injection into a theorem prover.
The hard part: Natural language is inherently ambiguous, whereas theorem provers require rigid, infallible logic. LLMs are currently used to extract relationships, but they hallucinate constraints that make the background theory internally inconsistent. 
Work required: A heavy, multi-month engineering effort requiring the chaining of modern Agentic LLMs (like Qwen or Claude) with formal verification systems (like Lean or Z3) to iteratively debug the generated axioms before they are passed to the Symbolic Regression module. The fact that the AI-Descartes team explicitly stated they rely on human experts to write these axioms proves this is a real, standing gap (cite: 45).

2. Multi-Modal Latent to Symbolic Bridges
What goes in: Raw video or high-dimensional sensor arrays (e.g., a video of a double pendulum).
What comes out: Extracted, decoupled scalar variables (angles, velocities) fed directly into PySR.
The hard part: Disentangling latent representations in autoencoders so they map 1-to-1 with physical reality. Several groups are trying to build this privately by forcing neural networks to output low-dimensional bottleneck vectors before running SR, but there is no canonical, plug-and-play community standard.

PART 7. NEGATIVE RESULTS, FAILED PROGRAMMES, AND STANDING CRITIQUES

The history of this field is littered with methods that claimed to have automated discovery but were actually measuring an artifact of human intervention or memorization.

The Standing Critique of BACON (and Early Heuristics):
The foundational critique of Langley's BACON, leveled by historians of science and contemporary computer scientists, is that BACON did not "discover" anything; it merely curve-fit data that had been meticulously pre-processed by human operators (cite: 46, 48). The humans implicitly understood the domain and filtered the dataset to include only the relevant variables (e.g., planetary distance and period). Furthermore, BACON relied on holding variables constant, which is experimentally impossible in observational astronomy. This critique was never fully answered by the heuristic community, leading to the total abandonment of BACON's architecture in favor of modern genetic programming.

The LLM Memorization Failure:
Throughout 2023 and 2024, numerous preprints claimed that Large Language Models could perform zero-shot mathematical discovery and symbolic regression simply by prompting. This has been thoroughly debunked. The LLM-SRBench project demonstrated that LLMs were simply regurgitating equations from their training data (cite: 79). When standard equations (like the Feynman set) were mathematically transformed to look unfamiliar (LSR-Transform) or replaced with synthetic forms (LSR-Synth), LLM performance collapsed to 31.5 percent (cite: 80). Using an LLM as a direct mathematical discovery engine is a failed paradigm.

The Agentic "Tool Paradox":
Systems like The AI Scientist attempt to automate the entire workflow. However, recent evaluations (such as NEWTONBENCH) reveal a severe negative result: providing LLM agents with code interpreters and evaluation tools actually hinders highly capable models (cite: 57). The agents suffer from a premature shift from exploration to exploitation—they find a script that runs without crashing, declare victory, and write a shallow, hallucinatory paper, rather than digging for the optimal scientific truth. The $15 papers generated by these systems often contain citation errors, shallow experiments, and fabricated results (cite: 49).

Overfitting to FSReD:
The Feynman Symbolic Regression Database is effectively saturated. Methods that look incredibly strong on FSReD frequently fail to generalize to the real-world tracks of SRBench. FSReD is completely noiseless in its base form. Algorithms that lack robust parsimony scaling perfectly solve FSReD but devolve into chaotic curve-fitting on real data (cite: 16).

PART 8. WHERE AN ENTRANT SHOULD ACTUALLY AIM IN 2026

Given the saturated benchmarks and the failure of pure LLMs to do math, a well-resourced newcomer must exploit the exact intersection of GP search power and LLM semantic capabilities. Do not attempt to build a better Genetic Programming engine; Cranmer (PySR) and Burlacu (Operon) have already won that race. 

Ranked Experiments to Run Now:

1. Autonomous Axiom-Constrained Discovery
What to do: Combine a visual/textual LLM agent with the AI-Descartes reasoning module and PySR. Feed the LLM a collection of historical papers on a physical phenomenon and ask it to output Z3/Lean logic constraints. Pass these constraints and raw data to AI-Descartes.
Feasibility: Feasible now because LLMs (like GPT-4o or Claude 3.5) are finally competent at generating formal code/logic, whereas previous generations failed syntax completely. 
What it measures: The end-to-end success rate of fully autonomous, physically consistent law discovery without human axiom translation.
Falsification: If the LLM cannot consistently generate logically sound axioms without creating unresolvable contradictions in the theorem prover, the dream of zero-human-in-the-loop derivable discovery is falsified.

2. Multi-view Symbolic Regression (MvSR) for Universal Constant Extraction
What to do: Use the Operon framework to run MvSR across heterogeneous datasets of the same phenomenon taken under vastly different environmental conditions (e.g., fluid dynamics data from different temperatures and viscosities). Force the algorithm to find a single functional form, utilizing realization-specific parameters and universal class-parameters (cite: 72).
Feasibility: Feasible now due to recent updates in Operon and PySR supporting multi-dataset simultaneous optimization.
What it measures: The ability of SR to isolate true universal constants from environmental artifacts.
Falsification: If the Pareto front requires an entirely distinct functional form for each dataset rather than isolating a universal class-parameter, MvSR fails to generalize beyond isolated curve fitting.

3. The LLM Idea-Generator vs. GP Executor (The LaSR approach)
What to do: Replicate the LaSR methodology (cite: 74). Use an LLM not to generate equations, but to generate abstract scientific concepts or intermediate variables (feature engineering), and feed those constructed features into PySR.
Feasibility: High. LLMs excel at semantic abstraction (e.g., "combine mass and velocity into momentum"), while PySR excels at the combinatorial math.
What it measures: The reduction in search depth and CPU time when PySR is seeded with LLM-generated semantic priors versus a cold random start.
Falsification: If the LLM-seeded features do not significantly shift the Pareto front toward higher accuracy/simplicity compared to random mutation, then semantic prior injection is useless.

What Will NOT Work:
Do not attempt to prompt an LLM to look at a CSV file and output the governing equation. This will fail. The LLM will either guess a famous equation it memorized, or it will hallucinate a mathematically invalid string. The current frontier has proven that LLMs are semantic engines, not rigorous combinatorial optimizers. Use the LLM to orchestrate the pipeline and parse the background theory, but hand the actual equation discovery over to the deterministic, evolutionary brutality of PySR.
