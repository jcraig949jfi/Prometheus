PART 1. THE FIELD IN 2026, AND ITS FRONTIER

Artificial Chemistry in 2026 is an abstraction of chemical processes using formal rules to study emergence, self-organization, and the origin of life (cite: 6). Rather than simulating the quantum or thermodynamic realities of molecules, it treats molecules as algorithms or strings, and reactions as computations. The field was partially dormant and absorbed into Systems Biology and Artificial Life for a decade, losing its focus on pure abstract computation in favor of data-driven metabolic network analysis. However, between 2023 and 2026, a resurgence occurred, driven by massive increases in compute power and the application of Category Theory to formalize the stochastic dynamics of these abstract reactors (cite: 4, 66).

What is SETTLED: It is mathematically and experimentally settled that untyped lambda calculus terms, when allowed to collide and beta-reduce in a well-mixed stochastic reactor, will inevitably collapse into stable, self-maintaining organizations. The emergence of Level 0 (self-copying) and Level 1 (hypercycles) organizations is a robust, universal feature of these constructive dynamical systems (cite: 3). 

What is CONTESTED: The physical relevance of these models remains highly contested. Critics argue that unconstrained lambda calculus is merely grammar rewriting. Because beta-reduction can exponentially duplicate a term without consuming "energy" or "mass", opponents argue these systems violate the fundamental thermodynamic logic of living matter (cite: 33, 37). Proponents counter that the algebraic structure itself is the discovery, and physical constraints can be mapped onto it later.

What is OPEN: The emergence of Level 3 organizations, defined as the hierarchical composition of stable sub-organizations, is the primary open problem. Recent exhaustive computational runs have shown that Level 1 and Level 2 organizations resist higher-order combination (cite: 67). Breaking this complexity ceiling, potentially through typed lambda calculus or spatial topologies, is the active frontier.

PART 5. THE REPRODUCTION RECIPE

The single most reproducible and informative experiment is the emergence of Level 1 organizations (hypercycles) from random lambda terms, as executed in the 2024 Return to AlChemy study.

Exact software and version: Modern-AlChemy (mathis-group repository), 2024 release.
Exact dataset or generator: The built-in lambda-btree generator for random expression instantiation.
Parameters: 
Reactor population size: 1000 terms.
Step ceiling (reduction budget): 10000 steps per collision.
Collision regime: Random pair selection with replacement.
Number of independent replicates: 100 independent runs.
Seeding regime: Different pseudo-random number generator seeds for the initial lambda-tree generation and collision pairing.
Compute cost: Less than 10 CPU hours on a modern multi-core workstation.
Expected result: Within a few thousand collision generations, the reactor will collapse into a dynamically stable Level 1 organization. This state is defined by a sparse matrix of interacting terms that exclusively produce copies of themselves or each other.
Citation for comparison: Mathis et al., 2024, arXiv:2408.12137 (cite: 66).

Three most common ways people get this experiment wrong:
1. Generator Bias: Using a naive random string generator instead of a balanced abstract syntax tree generator for the lambda terms. This skews the initial pool heavily toward syntactically invalid or trivial terms, preventing the emergence of complex hypercycles.
2. Step Ceiling Miscalibration: Setting the reduction budget too low. Complex valid reactions will hit the ceiling, be incorrectly flagged as elastic, and fail to produce offspring, artificially suppressing the evolutionary pathway.
3. Strict Identity Matching: Failing to implement alpha-equivalence (variable renaming) when evaluating whether a normal form already exists in the reactor. Without alpha-equivalence, the system falsely registers identical functional terms as novel distinct species.

PART 6. WHAT DOES NOT EXIST AND WOULD HAVE TO BE BUILT

Categorical Execution Compiler:
The 2026 Pratt-Johns paper defined the theoretical framework for mapping algebraic artificial chemistry to Markov processes using a functor called "Flask". However, no off-the-shelf software exists that can take a Lawvere theory specification as input and automatically compile it into a high-performance stochastic simulation. You would have to build the interface that translates the categorical syntax into executable Rust structs. This is a massive undertaking requiring deep expertise in both Category Theory and systems programming.

Spatial Topology Router for Lambda Terms:
Modern-AlChemy models a perfectly well-mixed reactor. If you want to study how spatial boundaries affect lambda-term hypercycles, you must build a modular physics engine. The interface would require taking the outputs of the beta-reduction engine and applying diffusion logic, localized interaction constraints, and physical boundaries across a 2D or 3D grid or graph structure. Several groups have rebuilt private, hardcoded 2D grids for specific experiments, signaling a major gap for a generalized spatial plugin.

Strict Conservation Wrapper:
Lambda calculus inherently violates conservation laws because variable copying duplicates information and mass for free. No tool currently enforces strict thermodynamic constraints on pure lambda reduction. You would need to build a wrapper that dynamically tracks the abstract "mass" (node count of the AST) and "energy" (reduction steps) and penalizes or prevents collisions that violate a global conservation threshold.

PART 7. NEGATIVE RESULTS, FAILED PROGRAMMES, AND STANDING CRITIQUES

The Death Spike and Parasite Extinction:
In aspatial, well-mixed automata chemistries, a frequent negative result is the total collapse of the system due to parasites. A parasite is a term that exploits a replicator for its own replication but provides no catalytic benefit in return. In well-mixed systems like original Stringmol and early AlChemy setups, parasites inevitably emerge, exponentially outcompete the host replicators, and drive the entire reactor to extinction (the "death spike"). This proved that open-ended evolution is unstable without spatial compartmentalization to isolate parasitic outbreaks (cite: 43).

The Level 3 Complexity Roadblock:
A major failed programme is the assumption that given enough time and compute, well-mixed lambda reactors would naturally evolve Level 3 organizations (hierarchies of interdependent Level 2 organizations). The 2024 Mathis reproduction proved this false. While Level 1 and Level 2 organizations are highly robust against trivial collapse, they hit a hard complexity ceiling. They cannot easily combine into higher-order entities. The method looked strong but was mathematically saturated (cite: 67).

Standing Critiques of Physicality:
A foundational critique, initially raised by McMullin and echoed continuously by physical chemists, is that algorithmic chemistry is merely grammar rewriting. Because the model lacks physical constraints, phase barriers, and natural energy functions, the reactions are disconnected from physical reality. The critique argues that demonstrating self-organization in an unconstrained grammar space does not explain biological origins because biochemistry is fundamentally constrained by thermodynamics. This critique was partially answered by adding artificial energy fluxes and token limits to newer systems like Stringmol, but the core philosophical objection that abstract lambda terms are not chemistry remains unanswered and is a standing limit on the field (cite: 23, 33, 45).
