Key Points
Research suggests that the specific mechanism you described is formally known as Robust Intelligent Adaptive Curiosity, a method that successfully mitigated early issues with pure prediction-error rewards.
It seems likely that discrete region-splitting algorithms have been superseded by continuous statistical models and, more recently, by Unsupervised Environment Design.

Methodological Evolution
The specific mechanism you anchored to is historically accurate and foundational. It was introduced as Robust Intelligent Adaptive Curiosity by Baranes and Oudeyer [cite: 1, 2]. Your understanding of the mechanism is correct: computing the derivative of prediction error over time to estimate learning progress prevents the agent from being captured by the "noisy TV" problem—situations where error is high but unlearnable. However, carving the space into discrete regions via tree-splitting suffers from the curse of dimensionality. By 2019, this was largely superseded by Absolute Learning Progress with Gaussian Mixture Models, which fits statistical distributions over the space rather than hard-splitting it [cite: 3, 4]. 

The Modern Paradigm
Since 2023, the computational frontier has shifted from tabula rasa neural networks optimizing raw sensorimotor parameters toward foundation models optimizing semantic curricula [cite: 5, 6]. The core philosophy of learning progress remains, but the substrate has changed from continuous robotic parameters to discrete code, tool-use, and language [cite: 7].

PART 1. THE FIELD IN 2026, AND ITS FRONTIER

In 2026, the field of Intrinsic Motivation in artificial agents has largely bifurcated. One branch has been absorbed into Unsupervised Environment Design, a subfield of reinforcement learning focused on adversarial curriculum generation and minimax regret [cite: 8, 9]. The other, more dominant branch has merged into Foundation Model Scaffolding and Open-Ended Agentic Evolution [cite: 5, 6, 10]. The classical approach of training a tabula rasa deep reinforcement learning student via a continuous curriculum teacher (like Absolute Learning Progress with Gaussian Mixture Models) is dormant. What was lost in this merge is the focus on low-level sensorimotor discovery. Modern agents are given pre-trained language models and code-execution abilities, meaning they no longer have to discover basic physics or motor control from scratch. Instead, they use execution errors and language feedback as a proxy for learning progress [cite: 7, 11].

What is SETTLED: Pure prediction error is a flawed intrinsic reward. The field universally accepts that agents optimizing for pure error will become trapped by stochasticity (the noisy TV problem). Using the derivative of error (learning progress), or regret-based bounds, is mandatory for robust curriculum generation [cite: 1, 2, 12]. Furthermore, the necessity of dual-curriculum or teacher-student architectures is settled; the curriculum generator must be decoupled from the learning agent to prevent catastrophic forgetting and to maintain a stable learning trajectory [cite: 13].

What is CONTESTED: The optimal metric for curriculum advancement. On one side, researchers advocating for Unsupervised Environment Design argue for Minimax Regret, where a teacher algorithm intentionally creates environments that maximize the difference between the student's current performance and the theoretical optimal performance [cite: 8]. On the other side, classical Intrinsic Motivation researchers argue for Absolute Learning Progress, claiming that adversarial minimax approaches often generate impossibly hard environments that crush the student's policy, whereas learning progress naturally scales difficulty to the student's exact frontier [cite: 3, 4]. 

What is OPEN: Open-ended self-modification. The frontier is currently focused on systems like the Darwin Gödel Machine, which attempts to bridge the gap between empirical learning progress and recursive self-improvement [cite: 10, 14, 15]. It remains an open problem how to rigorously measure learning progress when the agent's underlying architecture, not just its policy, is continuously self-modifying through code generation.

PART 5. THE REPRODUCTION RECIPE

The single most reproducible and informative experiment to understand the mechanics of learning progress is the evaluation of Absolute Learning Progress with Gaussian Mixture Models on the parametric BipedalWalker environment.

Exact software and version: Use the `flowersteam/teachDeepRL` repository [cite: 3]. You must construct a Docker container explicitly running Python 3.6, PyTorch 1.x, and OpenAI Gym 0.10.9 [cite: 3, 24]. 

Dataset or generator: The parameterized BipedalWalker environment provided within the `teachDeepRL` repository. The parameter space controls the stump height and pit gap width of the terrain [cite: 3, 17].

Parameters to set:
Teacher algorithm: ALP-GMM.
Student algorithm: Soft Actor-Critic.
Number of Gaussian components: dynamically determined by the fit, but initialized with a maximum capacity of 50.
Epsilon (random exploration): 0.1 to 0.2 [cite: 4].
Student network size: Standard multi-layer perceptron (two layers of 256 units).

Replicates and seeding: You must run 15 to 20 independent replicates. Seed the environment, the teacher, and the student independently [cite: 4].

Compute cost: Approximately 200 to 300 CPU hours per replicate. Box2D physics simulation on CPU is the bottleneck; GPU acceleration for the Soft Actor-Critic network provides minimal speedup due to simulation overhead [cite: 4, 9].

Expected result: The teacher should initially sample parameters randomly. As the Soft Actor-Critic student fails on difficult terrain (pit gaps too wide) and succeeds on flat terrain, learning progress zeroes out at both extremes. The teacher will converge its sampling distribution onto the "frontier" of difficulty (e.g., small stumps), gradually moving to harder terrain as the student masters it. You should compare against the learning curves in Portelas et al. 2019, expecting a cumulative reward curve that drastically outperforms uniform random sampling [cite: 4, 13].

Three most common ways people get this wrong:
First, failing to decouple the teacher's evaluation window from the student's gradient updates. If the student updates its weights mid-evaluation, the learning progress calculation becomes a mathematical artifact rather than a true derivative of competence.
Second, using insufficient independent replicates. Curriculum learning in Deep Reinforcement Learning has massive variance; fewer than 10 seeds will yield statistically meaningless results.
Third, compiling the Box2D physics engine with non-deterministic floating-point flags, which breaks reproducibility across different hardware architectures.

PART 6. WHAT DOES NOT EXIST AND WOULD HAVE TO BE BUILT

If you are entering this field in 2026, you will immediately notice a severe tooling gap: there is no off-the-shelf, hardware-accelerated interface that natively bridges Continuous Curriculum Teachers with Batched GPU Simulators.

What goes in: A batched tensor of environment parameters (e.g., 10000 parallel instances of varying friction, gravity, and terrain geometries).
What comes out: A batched tensor of episodic returns, which is ingested by a vectorized Gaussian Mixture Model or Unsupervised Environment Design teacher, which then outputs the next tensor of 10000 environment parameters.
The hard part: Current libraries like Brax or GPUDrive are built for domain randomization—they expect parameters to be drawn from a static, uniform distribution on the GPU [cite: 9]. Classical teacher algorithms (like Absolute Learning Progress with Gaussian Mixture Models) are written in basic NumPy or SciPy, expecting sequential, single-threaded interactions. You would have to rewrite the Gaussian Mixture Model fitting and the EXP4 multi-armed bandit sampling logic entirely in JAX or PyTorch primitives so it can execute on the GPU without memory-transfer bottlenecks.
How much work it is: A competent computational scientist fluent in JAX could build this bridge in four to six weeks of dedicated engineering. Several autonomous driving research groups (like the authors of Curriculum Learning for Autonomous Driving) have rebuilt specialized versions of this for their specific simulators, but a general-purpose Gymnasium-compliant API for GPU-native curriculum teachers does not exist [cite: 9].

PART 7. NEGATIVE RESULTS, FAILED PROGRAMMES, AND STANDING CRITIQUES

The noisy TV problem and pure prediction error: Early attempts in the 2000s and 2010s focused on maximizing prediction error. The fatal flaw, heavily documented in the literature, is that an agent maximizing error will stare endlessly at a source of irreducible noise (like a TV displaying static), because the prediction error never falls. This failed programme directly motivated the shift to learning progress (the derivative of error), which successfully drops to zero for both mastered tasks and irreducible noise [cite: 1, 2].

The curse of dimensionality in region-splitting: The exact mechanism you anchored to—Robust Intelligent Adaptive Curiosity, which splits the sensorimotor space into discrete regions—was shown to fail catastrophically as the dimensionality of the parameter space increases. Once the environment requires more than 10 to 12 parameters to define (for example, the 12-dimensional Hexagon Tracks environment), the discrete KD-tree splitting mechanism cannot gather enough statistical evidence in each sub-region to accurately compute learning progress. The regions become too sparse. This method was fundamentally retired in favor of continuous models like Absolute Learning Progress with Gaussian Mixture Models, which gracefully handles irrelevant dimensions [cite: 3, 4].

The failure of Unsupervised Environment Design in open-ended learning: Methods like Minimax Adversarial Training and Dual Curriculum Design promised mathematically rigorous curricula by optimizing regret. However, in highly stochastic environments, the adversarial teacher consistently exploits the student by generating mathematically valid but physically impossible environments. This forces the student into a defensive, conservative policy rather than an exploratory one [cite: 8].

The tabula rasa critique: A standing methodological critique of the entire field of intrinsic motivation is that the "intrinsic" nature of the learning is an illusion. Critics argue that human engineers meticulously design the parameter space (e.g., bounding the maximum width of a pit, or the types of objects in a room) to ensure the learning progress gradient is smooth [cite: 1, 2, 10]. If the parameter space is entirely unstructured, learning progress algorithms often fail to find the frontier and degrade to random search. This critique was never fully answered by the sensorimotor community; instead, it was bypassed by the field's migration to Foundation Models, where the "structure" is embedded in the pre-trained language model rather than the physics engine [cite: 5, 7].

PART 8. WHERE AN ENTRANT SHOULD ACTUALLY AIM IN 2026

Given the computational rot of the older continuous-space codebases and the explosion of language-driven agents, a well-resourced newcomer with compute and coding ability should target the intersection of these two paradigms.

Experiment 1: Batched Learning Progress for GPU-Native Physics Simulators (Highest Rank)
What it is: Port the Absolute Learning Progress with Gaussian Mixture Models algorithm into a pure JAX implementation, and attach it to a massive GPU-parallelized physics simulator like Brax or GPUDrive. 
Feasibility: Feasible now because batched reinforcement learning simulators have finally matured, solving the throughput bottleneck that plagued CPU-bound environments like Box2D [cite: 9].
What it measures: The wall-clock time and sample efficiency required to master a highly complex, high-dimensional robotic task compared to uniform domain randomization.
Falsification: The idea is falsified if the compute overhead of fitting the Gaussian Mixture Models on the GPU outpaces the sample-efficiency gains of the curriculum, making naive random search faster in wall-clock time.

Experiment 2: Learning Progress for Code-Generation Skill Libraries (Second Rank)
What it is: Replace Voyager's heuristic-based task proposal prompt with a formal learning progress mathematical model. Instead of relying on a Large Language Model to guess what skill to learn next, track the success rate and execution errors of generated code snippets over time. Compute the empirical derivative of competence for different programming concepts, and force the agent to write programs in the regions of highest progress.
Feasibility: Feasible now because we have robust programmatic evaluation harnesses (like SWE-bench) and reliable structured outputs from models [cite: 10].
What it measures: The rate of accumulation of verifiable skills in a code library, compared against classical heuristic prompting.
Falsification: Falsified if the Large Language Model's zero-shot planning capabilities are already so strong that enforcing a mathematical curriculum degrades performance by artificially constraining the agent's exploration.

What will NOT work:
Attempting to scale the classical Robust Intelligent Adaptive Curiosity (discrete region-splitting) method to raw pixel spaces or latent dimensions of diffusion models. The geometric structure of raw pixels lacks the smooth gradients of difficulty required for learning progress to be computable [cite: 1]. Splitting a region of pixel space based on prediction error variance will result in statistically meaningless partitions. If you do not have a pre-defined, low-dimensional semantic parameter space, classical sensorimotor intrinsic motivation algorithms will fail entirely.
