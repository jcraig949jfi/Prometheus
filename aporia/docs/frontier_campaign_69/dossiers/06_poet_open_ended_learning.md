# FRONTIER PRACTITIONER DOSSIER
Field: POET and Open-Ended Learning

Leading Summary

This report is written for a competent computational scientist entering the field of open-ended learning and algorithm-environment co-evolution in 2026. Your understanding of the Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer method is historically accurate and captures the core mechanics of the 2019-2020 era. However, the frontier has fundamentally shifted over the last three years. The bottleneck in open-ended learning is no longer the reinforcement learning algorithm itself, but the expressiveness of the environment parameterisation and the computational overhead of evaluating transfer across populations. 

Today, the field is dominated by two major paradigm shifts. First, the parameterisation of environments has moved from numerical arrays (like terrain roughness) to executable code generated by Large Language Models, allowing semantic mutations that introduce entirely new rules and reward functions. Second, the execution of these environments has moved from CPU-bound Python loops to end-to-end vectorised GPU implementations using frameworks like JAX, reducing experimental iteration time from weeks to hours. 

PART 1. THE FIELD IN 2026, AND ITS FRONTIER

Your description of the Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer method is structurally precise and conceptually correct. The mechanism of mutating environment parameters subject to a minimal criterion, coupled with periodic bipartite transfer evaluations, remains the canonical template for open-ended learning. However, if you build a system exactly as you described it today, you will hit a ceiling within days. The field has realised that when environments are defined by fixed numerical parameters, the system eventually exhausts the combinatorics of those parameters and plateaus. 

In 2026, open-ended learning is the study of AI-Generating Algorithms that continuously invent increasingly complex tasks and their solutions without human intervention. 

What is SETTLED:
The necessity of co-evolution is settled. Static datasets and fixed environments inevitably lead to capability saturation. It is also settled that transfer learning is the load-bearing pillar of the method; skills acquired in adjacent environments act as stepping stones to solve target environments that are unreachable via direct gradient descent. Finally, it is settled that traditional reinforcement learning environments written in standard Python are too slow for population-based co-evolution. Hardware-accelerated, end-to-end vectorised environments are now mandatory for serious experimental work.

What is CONTESTED:
The definition and measurement of open-endedness are heavily contested. The artificial life community traditionally defines open-endedness as the continuous generation of artefacts that are both novel and learnable. However, the information-theoretic community argues that generating novel and learnable policies does not guarantee the agent is acquiring genuinely useful information. There is a live disagreement over whether current Large Language Model-driven environment generators are truly open-ended, or if they are merely interpolating the finite training data of the underlying foundation model. 

What is OPEN:
The creation of an evaluator or minimal criterion that scales infinitely without requiring a human-in-the-loop is an open problem. When environments become semantic and code-based, determining if a newly generated task is "not too easy and not too hard" becomes computationally explosive. Furthermore, maintaining structural invariants and preventing memory leaks during the procedural generation of infinite tasks remains a purely open software engineering challenge. 

What changed in the last three years:
Between 2023 and 2026, the field moved away from hand-crafted procedural generation (such as mutating Compositional Pattern Producing Networks to draw 2D terrains) and adopted Large Language Models as the mutation operators. Systems like Eureka and OMNI-EPIC now prompt foundation models to write novel environment code and reward functions directly. Simultaneously, the underlying simulators were rewritten in JAX, allowing thousands of environments and agents to be simulated in parallel on a single GPU.

This introduces Protagonist Antagonist Induced Regret Environment Design. It reframes the minimal criterion as a regret-maximisation game between a teacher proposing environments and two agents (a protagonist and an antagonist) trying to solve them.

PART 5. THE REPRODUCTION RECIPE

The single most reproducible and informative experiment to run as a newcomer is the baseline evaluation of Proximal Policy Optimization on the Craftax-Classic environment using the PureJaxRL framework. This will calibrate your compute, prove your JAX toolchain is working, and give you the exact baseline against which modern open-ended algorithms are judged.

Software: Python 3.10, JAX version 0.6.0, Craftax version 1.6.0, and the PureJaxRL training loop from the Craftax repository.
Dataset/Generator: The Craftax-Classic procedural environment generator.
Parameters: Use the hyperparameter defaults defined in the Matthews et al. 2024 paper. Specifically: learning rate 0.0001, minibatch size 2048, and the mixed Hindsight Experience Replay mode disabled.
Replicates: 5 independent replicates.
Seeding: Standard JAX PRNGKey splitting, seeds 0 to 4.
Compute cost: Approximately 1 hour total on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 or 4090 GPU to reach one billion environment interactions.
Expected result: The agent should achieve roughly 90 percent of the optimal reward on the Craftax-Classic achievements. This corresponds to the baseline published in Matthews et al., ICML 2024, PMLR 235.

The three most common ways people get this experiment wrong:
1. Failing to wrap the environment in the AutoResetEnvWrapper. Because JAX environments do not halt, an episode that finishes will continue stepping into out-of-bounds memory or invalid game states unless explicitly reset via a boolean mask.
2. Breaking the JIT compilation. If you inject Python side-effects, print statements, or non-JAX arrays into the training loop to monitor the minimal criterion, JAX will fall back to the CPU, destroying the 250x speedup and making the experiment take weeks.
3. Texture caching conflicts. If you modify the procedural generation rules of Craftax but do not clear the local texture cache, the renderer will mismatch the underlying collision grid, leading you to believe the agent is failing to learn when in fact it is navigating an invisible maze.

PART 6. WHAT DOES NOT EXIST AND WOULD HAVE TO BE BUILT

If you want to run a frontier experiment in 2026, you will discover that a hardware-accelerated, GPU-native POET orchestrator does not exist off-the-shelf. 

Currently, libraries like PureJaxRL provide the inner loop: they can train an agent on an environment blindingly fast. However, the outer loop of POET involves generating candidate environments, evaluating the minimal criterion, and executing the all-to-all bipartite transfer evaluation. 

In existing setups, researchers run the inner RL loop on the GPU, pull the agent weights and environment parameters back to the CPU, run the evolutionary mutations in Python, and then push the new pairs back to the GPU. This CPU-GPU communication overhead destroys the advantages of hardware acceleration.

You will have to build an entirely JAX-compiled outer loop. 
Interface: 
Input: A tensor of environment parameters of shape "population size, parameter dimensions" and a tensor of agent weights.
Output: An updated tensor of environments that have passed the minimal criterion, and an updated tensor of agent weights after transfer replacements.
The hard part: Implementing the transfer evaluation requires taking every agent and running it in every environment. In JAX, this requires writing a double vmap (vectorising over agents and vectorising over environments) to construct an evaluation matrix, followed by an argmax to replace incumbent weights. Doing this without running out of GPU VRAM requires careful gradient checkpointing and memory management. It is roughly one month of difficult engineering work. The fact that multiple labs have rebuilt this privately (often poorly) is the strongest signal of a real tooling gap in the field.

PART 7. NEGATIVE RESULTS, FAILED PROGRAMMES, AND STANDING CRITIQUES

The most critical negative result regarding your anchored method is that the Protagonist Antagonist Induced Regret Environment Design algorithm, which attempted to formalise the minimal criterion mathematically as regret, was fundamentally unstable. The objective function was nonconvex-nonconcave. Wiatrak et al. (2020) and subsequent optimisation papers demonstrated that without a strongly concave reformulation, the adversarial teacher often collapsed, producing worst-case unsolvable environments rather than a smooth curriculum.

Furthermore, the standing methodological critique of the entire field comes from the information-theoretic community, specifically Xu, Zhu, and Van Roy (2026). The classic definition of open-endedness claims that a system is open-ended if it perpetually generates artefacts that are novel and learnable. Xu et al. mathematically proved that this is insufficient. They demonstrated that agents can continually generate novel and learnable policies without actually acquiring any information that enables improved performance. They introduced the bit-equivalent metric, proving that classical bandit environments and environments with unbounded rewards are not open-ended. If you rely purely on measuring diversity and novelty, you are measuring an artefact of the generator, not the intelligence of the system.

In the realm of Large Language Model-driven environments, Guerra and Hu (2026) issued a standing critique of open-ended evaluation. They proved that reducing open-ended generation to a single scalar score throws away most of the signal. More dangerously, they demonstrated severe evaluator conformity and anchor bias when language models evaluate open-ended tasks. If you use a foundation model to judge whether a generated environment passes the minimal criterion, the judge will shift its ratings based on peer context, often dragging the frontier toward mediocrity. This critique has not been fully answered; the field currently accepts evaluator noise as a cost of doing business.

Finally, the 2024 critique that foundation models themselves are a negative example of open-endedness stands unrefuted. Because foundation models are trained on static internet datasets, they will eventually plateau once the agent can model the entire dataset. They cannot generate their own novel explanatory knowledge outside their training distribution. Relying on them as the sole mutation engine places a hard upper bound on the open-endedness of your system.

PART 8. WHERE AN ENTRANT SHOULD ACTUALLY AIM IN 2026

Experiment 1: Goal-Conditioned All-Goals Learning inside JAX.
Instead of maintaining a population of isolated agent-environment pairs and evaluating transfer periodically, you should implement an architecture that learns everything at once. Build a system where a single, massive neural controller is conditioned on the semantic parameters of the generated environments. Every time a new environment is generated, evaluate the agent's trajectory not just on the current environment, but retrospectively on all previously archived environments simultaneously.
Feasibility: This is feasible now because of the Learning Everything at Once framework introduced by Matthews et al. in 2026, which allows efficient parallel all-goals updates with a single pass through the network, achieving a 250x speedup over naive relabelling.
Measurement: You would measure the zero-shot success rate of the agent on held-out, human-designed environments compared to an agent trained purely via the traditional POET transfer mechanism. 
Falsification: The idea is falsified if the goal-conditioned agent suffers from catastrophic interference, where learning a new extreme environment degrades its performance on historical stepping-stones faster than the all-goals update can correct it.

Experiment 2: The Bit-Equivalent POET.
Rebuild the POET minimal criterion using the information-theoretic bit-equivalent metric rather than simple threshold scoring. Instead of admitting an environment because its score is "not too high and not too low", admit it only if the minimum amount of useful information required to attain expected reward on that environment forces the agent's bit-equivalent to grow linearly.
Feasibility: Feasible due to the recent mathematical formalisation by Xu et al. in 2026.
Measurement: You would measure the asymptotic rate of the agent's bit-equivalent growth over time.
Falsification: The idea is falsified if the environment generator cannot find a parameter space that sustains a linear growth rate, proving that the underlying simulator is fundamentally closed-ended regardless of the curriculum.

What will NOT work:
Attempting to scale up the original POET architecture by simply swapping the 2D bipedal walker for a complex 3D physics simulator, while keeping the procedural generation based on numerical arrays (like terrain heightmaps). This will fail because numerical procedural generation lacks the semantic depth required to escape saturation. Once the agent learns to walk over the maximum possible roughness, the minimal criterion will reject all new mutations, and the frontier will permanently stall. Semantic code generation is the only viable path forward.
