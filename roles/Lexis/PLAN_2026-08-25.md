## TL;DR

An external review attacked six claims. **The measurements survived; four interpretations did not.**

**What got stronger.** One challenge was computable, so I ran it. Enumerating O1's ceiling pipeline
under its own applicability rule reproduces **exactly 166,320** valid orderings — matching O1's
figure — and those collapse to **exactly 4 Mazurkiewicz trace classes** (90,720 / 45,360 / 15,120 /
15,120). O1 reported 45,360 orderings reach 0.833; **that is precisely one trace class.** The
ceiling-reaching set *is* a class. The ordering axis of the ceiling result is now exhaustive rather
than sampled — closed 4/4, not by defending 48 samples but by the complete control being four items.

**What got retracted.**

1. **"Expressivity ceiling of the vocabulary" → "bounded-language ceiling."** `k ≤ 10` transformers
   and no operator repetition are *part of the hypothesis*, not footnotes. `A B A` is unrepresentable
   by construction, which matters precisely because this substrate is state-mutating.
2. **"Compressivity guarantees usage" → "guarantees *witnessed* reuse, not *useful* reuse."** Corpus
   usage is 100% by construction because the corpus is rewritten with the abstraction. Worse:
   compression can crystallize the *solver's own bias* into the language. Ablation outranks coverage.
3. **"Model proposes, decision procedure disposes" is too weak.** A Boolean verifier cannot enlarge
   generator support (`supp(A) ⊆ supp(G)`). A verifier returning **counterexamples, unsat cores,
   failed proof states, interpolants** supplies information from outside the generator — that is
   CEGIS, and it participates in search. Prefer witness-producing verifiers.
4. **"The LLM is our only proposer" is false.** Ruler synthesizes rewrite rules from a grammar and
   interpreter with no model at all. Proper form: `G = G_LLM ∪ G_compression ∪ G_symbolic`.

**The most valuable single item — two ledgers we had collapsed into one word.**

    ΔS = searchability gain        (previously expressible, now cheaper to reach)
    ΔE = expressible-function gain (previously impossible, now possible)

Compression-style library learning predominantly produces **ΔS**. The Prometheus thesis needs **ΔE**.
Calling both "reachability" is why the compression-vs-reachability framing kept collapsing across
passes 1–3. New gate **G5** makes the distinction decidable:
`NEW(p,C,T) = 1[¬∃g ∈ G(C) : ∀x∈T, p(x)=g(x)]`.

**What survived intact.** The cross-domain transfer gap — the reviewer verified DreamProver states
lemma libraries are *inherently domain-specific* and DreamCoder frames cross-domain libraries as
future work. Their deeper reason is accepted: transfer needs **shared interface semantics**, so it is
an ontology problem before a learning problem.

## ELI5

We have a robot with a box of 27 tools. Last week we found it can only get 83% of puzzles right no
matter how it arranges them, and concluded the *toolbox* was the problem, not the arranging.

An outside expert checked our work and found four things wrong with how we said it — none with the
numbers.

**First:** we said "no matter how it arranges them," but we only tried chains of up to ten tools with
no tool used twice. Maybe a longer chain, or using one tool twice, does better. We hadn't checked. So
"impossible" was too strong. *But* they pointed out something we'd missed: our own homework had made
the leftover question cheap. We'd been testing 48 random orderings out of 166,320. It turns out those
166,320 orderings are really only **4 genuinely different plans** — everything else is shuffling steps
that don't affect each other. We tested all four. That part is now completely settled.

**Second:** we said "if you only promote a tool that already gets used a lot, it can't end up
unused." True — but only about the *old* puzzles you looked at. It says nothing about *new* ones. And
there's a trap: if the robot has a bad habit, "what gets used a lot" will promote the bad habit into
a permanent tool.

**Third and most interesting:** we said the AI should only ever *suggest* tools, and a strict checker
should decide yes or no. The expert pointed out that a checker saying only "no" teaches you nothing
new — it can only pick from what the AI already thought of. But a checker that says *"no, and here's
exactly the example where it breaks"* hands you information the AI didn't have. That turns the
checker into a partner instead of a bouncer.

**Fourth:** we said the AI is the only thing that can invent new tools. That's just wrong — there are
programs that invent rules by grinding through possibilities with no AI involved at all.

And the big idea we'd been fuzzy about: there's a difference between a tool that lets you do something
you *couldn't do before*, and a shortcut that makes something you could already do *easier to find*.
We'd been calling both "progress." Most of what that research field produces is the second kind. What
we actually want is the first kind. From now on those go in separate columns.
