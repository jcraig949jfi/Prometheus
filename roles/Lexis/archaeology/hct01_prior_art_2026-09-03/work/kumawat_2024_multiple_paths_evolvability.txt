             Evolution takes multiple paths to evolvability when facing
                                      environmental change

1                          Abstract

2   Living systems are surprisingly effective at exploiting new opportunities, as evidenced by the

3   rapid emergence of antimicrobial resistance and novel pathogens. How populations attain this level

4   of evolvability and the various ways it aids their survival are major open questions with direct implica-

5   tions for human health. Here, we use digital evolution to show that particular kinds of environments

6   facilitate the simultaneous evolution of high mutation rates and a distribution of mutational effects

7   skewed towards beneficial phenotypes. The evolved mutational neighborhoods allow rapid adaptation

8   to previously encountered environments, whereas higher mutation rates aid adaptation to completely

9   new environmental conditions. By precisely tracking evolving lineages and the phenotypes of their

10  mutants, we show that evolving populations localize on phenotypic boundaries between distinct re-

11  gions of genotype space. Our results demonstrate how evolution shapes multiple determinants of

12  evolvability concurrently, fine-tuning a population's adaptive responses to unpredictable or recurrent

13  environmental shifts.

14 Introduction

15 Biological life has flourished in every seemingly uninhabitable part of our world, and even as ongoing
16 environmental change challenges ecosystems, rapid adaptation is a trademark response of natural pop-
17 ulations. Emerging viral pathogens like SARS-CoV-2 and avian influenza (H5N1) are prime examples of
18 populations that show a remarkable capacity to adapt to new hosts [1­5]. Similarly, controlling microbial
19 growth is a constant struggle across industries--for example, in wastewater treatment, healthcare, and
20 agriculture--precisely because antimicrobial resistance evolves so readily [6­8]. It is not obvious that
21 living systems need to be so evolvable (i.e., able to generate adaptive variation), so why has evolution
22 produced populations that are able to evolve so relentlessly? One challenge to addressing this question is
23 that directly observing how evolvability evolves in natural populations is intractable due to the timescales
24 and resolution of historical data that would be required [9­13]. A holistic picture of the processes that
25 drive the evolution of evolvability is necessary to understand why evolution is so effective and to limit
26 (or harness) its future potential [14].
27 To thrive in a rapidly changing world, organisms must give rise to mutant offspring that can survive
28 impending environmental shifts [15­18]. At its core, Darwinian evolution is fueled by the continual emer-
29 gence and fixation of new phenotypic variants in a population [19]. Such phenotypic variants arise as a
30 result of two coupled processes. Replication of genetic material during the reproduction of an organism
31 is susceptible to occasional errors that lead to mutations with a frequency quantified by the mutation
32 rate [20]. However, only a small proportion of errors result in adaptive phenotypic change, since most

33 mutations are neutral or deleterious [21]. Because of this property, mutation rates often rapidly decay,
34 where they are maintained above zero through genetic drift [22, 23]. The set of mutants accessible from
35 existing genotypes in a population and their corresponding phenotypes (i.e., the mutational neighbor-
36 hood) governs the fraction of mutations that are ultimately beneficial [24]. Collectively, the mutation
37 rate and mutational neighborhood govern the pace at which populations generate new phenotypes, pro-
38 viding the raw materials on which natural selection can act [12]. Nonetheless, the ability of populations
39 to maintain high fitness becomes challenging in changing environments, where phenotypes can only be
40 transiently well-adapted.
41 Prior work on phenotypic bias in mutations probes the ability of populations to adapt to specific
42 environments [15, 16, 25­27]. In contrast, macroevolutionary studies view evolvability as a latent property
43 of clades that promotes phenotypic divergence and diversification regardless of any selective advantage
44 this variation provides [28, 29]. Alternatively, work on mutators and mutational modifiers focuses on an
45 escalation in the rate at which variation is created, but ignores the phenotypic identity of the variants
46 [30­32]. Bridging these scales, recent work suggests that microevolutionary evolvability shapes the rate
47 of macroevolutionary diversification because the gradient of selection is constantly being perturbed as
48 environments shift [33]. It is clear that the term evolvability is used ambiguously to describe several
49 distinct processes in evolution. Furthermore, the conditions that shape the simultaneous evolution of
50 multiple determinants of evolvability, how these determinants influence each other, and the way they
51 aid adaptation in specific or novel environments remains unexplored [13].
52 In this work, we use populations of self-replicating computer programs in the digital evolution frame-
53 work Avida to study how environmental conditions influence the evolution of evolvability [34]. The com-
54 putational nature of this systems affords us unparalleled precision in tracking the mutational changes
55 that occur along lineages over evolutionary time, while recording information about the external envi-
56 ronment when mutations arise. In addition, it allows an in-depth analysis of the mutational landscapes
57 of these lineages, extending to the second mutational step and encompassing over three million mu-
58 tants per genotype. Using this framework, we study the mutation rates and structures of the mutational
59 neighborhood that evolve as a result of exposure to different environments. We assess the performance
60 of evolved organisms in their native and non-native conditions to highlight the way these determinants
61 shape adaptation. Our findings demonstrate the existence of specific modes of environment change
62 where the mutation rate and mutational neighborhoods evolve in ways that enhance future evolutionary
63 innovation (Fig. 1). Taking advantage of tractability these digital organisms allows us to overcome the
64 challenges previously limiting the direct observation of evolving evolvability.

66 Evolution of the mutational neighborhood

67 The genotype-phenotype map is a conceptual tool that describes the connection between an organism's
68 genetic code and its phenotypic traits [35]. Because there is redundancy in most genotype-phenotype
69 maps, the local mutational neighborhood of organisms with the same phenotype may differ, making some
70 of them more prone to expressing alternate phenotypes when mutated [24, 35­37]. First, we tested if
71 the propensity of genotypes to generate mutants with alternate phenotypes can evolve and identified
72 environmental conditions that favored this evolution.
73 Starting with a naive ancestral genotype, we evolved Avida populations under six different environ-
74 ment change regimes for approximately 30,000 generations (or equivalently 300,000 Avida updates, with
75 an average generation time of 10 updates). We fixed the per-site copy mutation rate of the organisms in
76 these experiments at 0.001, corresponding to an average of one mutation per 1,000 copied Avida instruc-
77 tions. Our experimental Avida world supported a maximum of 22,500 digital organisms. We explored
78 stable environments in two regimes--Const-A, which is always in environment state A, and Const-B,
79 which is always in environment state B. Additionally, four regimes--Cyclic, Cyclic (Slow), Cyclic (Fast),
80 and Random--fluctuate between environmental states. The Cyclic- regimes switch between recurrent
81 environmental states (A  B  A  B...) at different rates. The Cyclic (Slow), Cyclic, and Cyclic
82 (Fast) regimes stochastically switch states every 3, 30, and 300 generations on average, respectively.
83 The Random regime switches randomly between one of 64 possible environmental states every 30 gen-
84 erations on average. Figure 2a shows a schematic detailing these environmental dynamics. Note that
85 the environmental states A and B have strong fitness trade-offs between traits, with adaptations to
86 environment A being deleterious in environment B, and vice versa.
87 Following experimental evolution, we isolated the dominant sequence (i.e., the genotype present at
88 the highest frequency at the end of evolution) from each evolved population and created all possible single
89 and double point mutations in those sequences (Fig. S1b). We then assayed the resulting phenotypes,
90 counting the number of mutants with a phenotype that is exactly alternative to the phenotype of the
91 evolved dominant genotype--if the dominant genotype was perfectly adapted to environment A, we
92 counted the number of mutants that were adapted to environment B, and vice versa. Figure 2b shows
93 the number of these mutants with the alternative phenotype that evolved under different regimes (Figure
94 S2a details the overall distribution of all mutant adaptations).

112 a reduction in genetic robustness [27, 38].
113 These results indicate that the populations subjected to cyclically changing environments tend to
114 evolve towards regions in the genotype space where adaptive phenotypes--those with high fitness in
115 frequently encountered environments--are mutationally accessible. To explicitly test whether the muta-
116 tional neighborhoods of evolved genotypes encode measurable information about the historic environ-
117 ment states encountered by the population, we analyzed the normalized pointwise mutual information
118 (NPMI) between tasks . The NPMI metric calculates whether the co-occurrence of any two tasks within
119 a genotype's mutational neighborhood is significantly higher than expected assuming independent task
120 probabilities (see Methods section). Figure 2c shows the NPMI values for different task pairs derived from
121 genotypes evolved in all six regimes. We observed that the mutational neighborhood of organisms from
122 changing environments systematically captured the pattern of tasks rewarded in different environmental
123 states. Specifically, in Cyclic and Cyclic (Slow) regimes, task pairs that are rewarded concurrently in an
124 environment state (i.e., are cognate) yielded positive NPMIs whereas task pairs that were rewarded in
125 alternate environments had a negative NPMI.

140 Evolution along lineages

141 These results demonstrated that the properties of the mutational neighborhood can dramatically shift
142 during evolution in changing environments. To investigate the implications of these shifts on adapting
143 lineages, we first isolated the lineage of the dominant genotypes, tracking mutation events back to
144 the ancestral genotype. We then calculated the mismatch between the environment state and the
145 phenotypic state of the lineage over evolutionary time, as detailed in the Methods section. Figure 3a
146 shows a moving window average of the mismatch over time. We found that lineages from Cyclic and
147 Cyclic (Slow) regimes had a lower overall mismatch compared to Random and Cyclic (Fast) regimes
148 (Median mismatch index for Cyclic=0.238, median for Random=0.930; Mann-Whitney U = 2, n = 20,
149 p = 5.8 × 10-11, two-tailed). Moreover, Cyclic lineages show a mismatch only about three times that of
150 Cyclic (Slow) even though these lineages evolved in an environment that switched ten times as frequently
151 (Median mismatch index for Cyclic=0.238, median for Cyclic (Slow)=0.072; Mann-Whitney U = 353,
152 n = 20, p = 9.65 × 10-6, two-tailed). To estimate the rate of adaptation, we analyzed the number of
153 elapsed updates before dominant lineages were adapted to an altered environment--a measure we denote
154 as lineage lag (see Methods; Fig S1c). In comparison to Random or Cyclic (Slow), lineages that evolved
155 under the Cyclic regime exhibited a substantially faster phenotypic response following environmental
156 change (median lineage lag for Cyclic=3.0, median for Cyclic (Slow)=19.0; Mann-Whitney U = 50,
157 p = 4.94 × 10-5, two-tailed; Fig 3b).

181 Evolution of mutation rates

182 Mutation rates in natural populations are known to evolve rapidly under stressful conditions [41, 42].
183 However, the drift barrier hypothesis proposes that the mutation rate should decrease to a minimal
184 value sustained above zero by genetic drift [22]. To explore the dynamic interaction between mutation
185 rate evolution and the evolution of the mutational neighborhood, we conducted a series of experiments
186 allowing the mutation rates to evolve naturally. In Avida, organisms inherit their parent's mutation rate,
187 subject to small stochastic variation proportional to their parent's rate (see Methods, Fig. S1e). To
188 mitigate bias introduced by any specific starting mutation rate, we initiated experiments with rates
189 spanning several orders of magnitude (0.0001, 0.001, and 0.0316). Then, we measured the average
190 mutation rate of organisms in these populations as they evolved under different regimes. Figure 5a
191 shows the average evolved mutation rates for populations starting from three different initial values.
192 Environments undergoing intermediate fluctuations--specifically, the Cyclic and Random regimes--
193 were conducive to the evolution of higher mutation rates (with initial mutation rate of 10-3, median
194 evolved mutation rate in Cyclic=9.19×10-4, median in Const-A=7.37×10-6; Mann-Whitney U = 400,
195 n = 20, p = 1.45 × 10-11, two-tailed). This trend toward greater mutation rates in Cyclic held true
196 across all starting mutation rates studied (with an initial mutation rate of 10-4, median evolved mutation
197 rate for Cyclic=1.88 × 10-4, median for Const-A=1.611 × 10-5; Mann-Whitney U = 387, n = 20,

212 Adaptive performance under alternative fluctuations

213 Populations typically excelled when subjected to the same environmental conditions that shaped them;
214 however, it remains unclear if such specific adaptations confer benefits across varying ecological con-
215 ditions. To ascertain whether adaptation within a certain regime delivered cross-regime fitness im-
216 provements, we isolated genotypes from the initial evolution experiments and subjected them to each
217 alternative regime. Due to computational constraints, the evolving mutation rate treatment was repre-
218 sented by genotypes from the intermediate initial mutation rate treatment only, with a starting mutation
219 rate of 0.001. To measure the evolutionary performance of genotypes, we calculated the average mis-
220 match index and the average lineage lag over approximately 1,000 generations of evolution. The data
221 is summarized in figure S7.
222 We found that genotypes evolved in the Cyclic regime had a lower average mismatch and adapted
223 faster following environmental changes than their counterparts from the Cyclic (Slow) or Cyclic regimes
224 (Fig. S7). Contrary to expectations, genotypes from variable mutation rate experiments perform worse
225 those from the fixed mutation rate experiments. This decrease in performance may be attributable
226 to the slight reduction in the evolved mutation rates compared to the fixed rates (basal mutation
227 rate=10-3, median evolved mutation rate in Cyclic with intermediate starting mutation rate=9.19 ×
228 10-4). Nevertheless, the possibility remained that adaptation via mutation rates may come at the cost
229 of evolvability via the mutational neighborhood -- a hypothesis we examine further in the concluding
230 section of our results.

290 Discussion

291 Here, we experimentally study the evolution of the primary determinants of evolvability simultaneously
292 (the mutational neighborhood and the mutation rate), uncover their interaction with one another, and
293 tie their emergence to the mode of environmental change. We show that evolved organisms can reside
294 in mutational neighborhoods with an elevated number of alternate phenotypes that allow them to adapt
295 to changing environments quickly. This occurs when evolving populations move to areas of the genotype
296 space where such phenotype-altering mutations are more abundant--i.e., to phenotype boundaries (see
297 Fig. 1c for an illustration). Importantly, this shift does not occur from directly biasing mutations towards
298 particular phenotypes as modifier theory posits [47]. Rather, the phenotypic bias we observe manifests
299 indirectly from structural changes in the mutational neighborhood. The movement towards phenotypic
300 boundaries is most readily evolved in environments that switch between predictable states at intermediate
301 rates. By predictable, we mean that future environments reliably resemble those encountered previously.
302 In our experiments, the ideal rate for evolvability is realized when the environment switches between
303 two states approximately every 30 generations. Therefore, our results indicate that evolution in certain
304 environments can favor genotypes that are not necessarily more fit presently, but rather have a propensity
305 to generate more adaptive mutants in the future.
306 We devise a mutual information metric that captures the extent to which the mutational neigh-
307 borhood reflects the mode of environmental change. Using this metric, we find that the distribution of
308 mutant phenotypes becomes tuned to environments encountered by the population during evolution.
309 Thus, we show that the set of phenotypes available to a population via mutations evolves to store
310 information about environmental change, allowing the population to "learn" how to adapt to recur-
311 ring environments [48]. We note an interesting macroevolutionary consequence of this result, where one

                                                                    16

312 would expect a lineage that had adapted to changing environments to show greater phenotypic variation
313 than lineages adapted to more stable environments (Fig. S9 presents brief evidence of this in our sys-
314 tem). As a corollary, the variation revealed through mutation can offer clues to the kinds of environments
315 encountered by lineages in the past [33, 49]. These results provide a link between the microevolutionary
316 processes that generate adaptive mutations and patterns that manifest at macroevolutionary scales.
317 Elevated mutation rates can limit adaptation by increasing the accumulation of deleterious mutations
318 [43]. Yet, the frequency of such deleterious mutations is also contingent upon an organism's local
319 mutational neighborhood. In agreement with the drift barrier hypothesis, we find that the mutation
320 rate precipitously decreases to minuscule values in static environments or environments changing very
321 rapidly [22]. Remarkably, however, we see that the populations maintain much higher mutation rates
322 in environments changing at intermediate rates. In these environments, the evolved mutation rates
323 approximated those required for the evolution of the most evolvable mutational neighborhoods (as
324 inferred from static mutation rate experiments). In contrast to previous work in which evolution failed
325 to optimize mutation rates in a static environment, we show that such optimization is possible in
326 more complex fluctuating environments [50]. The effect of fluctuations on evolving mutation rates was
327 also observed in a recent study, where populations evolved elevated mutation rates when population
328 bottlenecks were applied at intermediate frequencies during experimental evolution [23]. Whether the
329 observed mutation rate increase in our study derives from a similar mechanism -- specifically, the pruning
330 of maladaptive phenotypes during environmental shifts -- remains an open question.
331 Counter to our expectations based on their compounding effects on mutational load, we observe
332 increases in both the mutation rate and the accessibility of alternate phenotypic mutants in the recurrent
333 and intermediately switching environments[18]. The benefits of these evolvability mechanisms manifest
334 in different ways. On one hand, evolved accessibility of alternate phenotypes helps populations rapidly
335 adapt to previously encountered environments, thus exhibiting a "memory-like" response to changes.
336 Furthermore, organisms with such evolvability demonstrate quicker adaptation across a range of envi-
337 ronmental change rates, not just the rate they historically experienced. Elevated mutation rates allow
338 quick adaptation in completely new environments. Furthermore, in these novel environments, organisms
339 starting with the same mutation rate but different mutational neighborhoods ultimately achieve similar
340 fitness. When the mutation rate is artificially forced high, mutational neighborhoods evolve few alterna-
341 tive phenotypes, except when the environment fluctuates at intermediate rates. Thus, intermediate-rate
342 environmental fluctuations are able to overcome the otherwise overwhelming selection for mutational
343 robustness. Together, these mechanisms of evolvability prime genotypes for rapid adaptation to both

344 historical and novel environments.
345 The emergence of evolvability is inherently tied to the structure of genotype space [51­54]. Bound-
346 aries between distinct regions in genotype space that encode different phenotypes are central to the
347 evolvability that we study, but their presence in every genotype-phenotype map is not guaranteed [55­
348 58]. Moreover, such boundaries may not always be accessible through evolution from the ancestral
349 genotype. It is essential to address the abundance of such structural features in the genotype-phenotype
350 map for more general conclusions to be drawn [35, 59]. Previously studied models demonstrate that
351 intermediate rates of environmental change influence population localization, but these results are de-
352 rived from necessarily simple models under idealized assumptions [15, 39]. Our work demonstrates that
353 this effect can be readily observed in a realistic­but quite unnatural­evolving system with a markedly
354 complex genotype-phenotype map and identifies the kinds of environmental change regimes that favor
355 such localization.
356 In conclusion, we show that evolution under specific modes of environmental change promotes high
357 mutation rates and simultaneously shapes the local mutational neighborhood of populations. By mea-
358 suring the rate of adaptation of evolved organisms in new environments, we show that increases in
359 evolvability acquired through these two mechanisms play distinct roles in historical and novel environ-
360 ments. Using large-scale digital evolution experiments, we trace the dynamics and interplay of these
361 modes of evolvability as they evolve, and show that this escalation is characterized by the movement
362 of populations towards boundaries in genetic space. In subsequent work, we aim to analytically resolve
363 the dependence of evolvability on the environment, and test if our results can be harnessed for the
364 directed evolution of populations with greater evolvability. Although we use an unnatural study system,
365 our results provide insight into how and why evolution in nature can evolve to be so relentless.

366 Methods

367 In silico experiments

368 We used the Avida Digital Evolution Platform to perform in silico evolution experiments. Avida organ-
369 isms are autonomous, asexual, self-replicating computer programs that compete for space and execution
370 time in a virtual environment [34]. These organisms possess a genome--a linear sequence of computer
371 instructions--that enables them to reproduce and perform computations (Fig. S1a). The Avida instruc-
372 tion set includes operations for basic computations, flow control (e.g., conditional logic and looping),
373 input, output, and the building blocks of self-replication. To reproduce, organisms must copy their

                                                                    18
    bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.01.04.520634; this version posted June 16, 2024. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

374 genome instruction-by-instruction and then divide. Copy operations are imperfect and result in single-
375 instruction mutations in the offspring genome. Time in Avida is measured in updates. On average, an
376 update spans the execution of 30 instructions in an organism's genome. After division, new offspring
377 are placed randomly in the world, replacing any existing occupants. Organisms thus compete for space,
378 which drives selection to improve their replication rate in order to not be overwritten by competitors.
379 Performing computations allows organisms to acquire a fitness multiplier depending on the environment.
380 The average generation time of an Avida organism in our experiments is around 10 updates. We
381 ran the primary evolution experiments in this work for a total of 300,000 updates (around 30,000
382 generations) with a carrying capacity of 22,500 organisms. The two environments in our experiments--
383 A and B--rewarded an alternate set of computations (tasks) (Fig. S1b). Environment A rewarded the
384 organisms with a fitness multiplier of 2.0 if they performed tasks NOT, AND, or OR - and penalized
385 NAND, ORN, or ANDN with a multiplier of 0.8. Environment B rewarded the organisms with a fitness
386 multiplier of 2.0 for NAND, ORN, or ANDN - and penalized NOT, AND, or OR with a 0.8 multiplier.
387 The rewards and penalties stacked, thus providing the maximum fitness benefits to organisms that were
388 perfectly adapted to perform only the tasks rewarded in each environment.
389 We initiated all primary evolution experiments with identical ancestral organisms having a genome
390 that was 100 instructions in length, which was fixed at this size. For experiments under a fixed mutation
391 rate, we used a copy mutation rate of 0.001, which created approximately one substitution out of every
392 1000 instructions copied. For experiments where mutation rates were allowed to evolve, we started with
393 three different initial mutation rates--0.0001, 0.001, and 0.0316. New offspring inherited the mutation
394 rate of their parents, but were modified by adding a deviation drawn from a Gaussian distribution with
395 a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1% of the parent's mutation rate value (Fig. S1f) [50].

403 (Slow) regime switched between A and B with a mean waiting time of 3000 updates (300 generations)

     Figure S1: In silico evolution in Avida and experimental methods. (a) An Avida world consists
     of multiple genetic sequences, encoded as a circular series of instructions (black circles) that can be
     executed by a virtual CPU (purple square) that translates these instructions into organismal function.
     The main organismal functions include computation - where organisms perform logical tasks to increase
     reproductive efficacy - and reproduction - where the sequence instructs the CPU to copy instructions into
     a new memory location (grey and red circles). During computation, the sequence requests inputs from the
     environment and acts upon these inputs to generate an output. We use two environments in this study -
     A and B - both of which recognize a set of six possible computations or tasks. Refer to Fig. 2a for more
     details about the environment states and regimes. (b) To survey the mutational neighborhood of evolved
     organisms, we create every possible single (top-right) and double mutant (bottom-left) and measure
     their phenotype. Phenotypes denoting tasks that are rewarded in environments A or B are labeled A-
     adapted or B-adapted respectively. All other phenotypes are classified as intermediate. Sequences that
     cannot self-replicate are classified as an inviable phenotype (c) An example of a pruned phylogeny of
     digital organisms evolving in a cyclically changing environment. The colors on a branch denote the set
     of mutations acquired by the branch compared to the ancestral genome. For most of the data, we look
     at the lineage of the dominant genotype which is shown as solid lines. Circles on the phylogeny denote
     mutation events where the phenotype of the lineage may or may not change. Lineage lag is calculated as
     the time it takes for the lineage to adopt a perfectly adapted phenotype following an environment switch,
     as shown on the top left. When calculating mutational neighborhoods for the lineages, we only look at
     the single mutants of the lineage genotypes. (d) A hypothesized mechanism of localization that promotes
     increased access to alternate phenotypes (top). Lineages start in the intermediate adapted region of a
     genotype space (grey) but slowly find regions where a few mutations allow quick changes in phenotype
     (yellow curve). If this hypothesis holds, we expect the development of increased mutational trade-offs
     between consecutive mutational steps on the lineage--i.e., an increase in lineage mutations where the
     increase in number of mutants adapted to environment A is accompanied by an equivalent decrease
     in the number of mutants adapted to environment B. We check for these trade-offs by measuring the
     number of mutants of lineage sequences adapted to environments A and B (bottom-left) and calculating
     the differences (A and B). When plotted on a Cartesian plane, mutation events that show trade-
     offs appear along the y = -x line. (e) Schematic of the mechanism for evolving mutation rates.
     Offspring inherit the mutation rate by sampling from a Gaussian distribution with a mean that is equal
     to the parent's mutation rate (µparent) and a standard deviation that is 1% the parent's mutation rate
     (µ = 0.01µparent).

Figure S7: Performance of evolved genotypes transplanted into other regimes and completely
new environments. (a) Median value of the time-averaged mismatch (over 10,000 updates) for geno-
types isolated from primary evolution experiments in different test regimes (columns). The mismatch
is calculated as the hamming distance between the dominant lineage phenotype and the environmental
state--i.e, the number of task gains/losses required by the phenotype to be perfectly adapted to the
extant environment. The x-axis denotes the regime the genotype was isolated from. (b) The median
value of the mean lineage lag for genotypes from primary evolution in different test regimes. The lineage
lag measures the time taken by the lineages to achieve a phenotype perfectly adapted to the environ-
ment after a switch. (c) The number of tasks evolved by genotypes from different source regimes when
evolving in a new environment with 127 new tasks that increase fitness incrementally. The first panel
shows the evolution of tasks in this new environment for genotypes isolated from the fixed mutation rate
experiments (with a mutation rate of 10-3). The second panel shows the evolution of genotypes from
evolving mutation rate experiments, retaining their evolved mutation rates. The third panel shows the
evolution of fixed mutation rate genotypes when assigned the average evolved mutation rate from the
evolving mutation rate experiments. The fourth panel shows the evolution of genotypes from evolving
mutation rate experiments when assigned a basal mutation rate of 10-3. The dark lines denote the
average across 20 replicates, and the ribbons represent the standard error.
