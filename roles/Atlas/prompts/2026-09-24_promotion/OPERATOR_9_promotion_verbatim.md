Yes — I think that is a better fit than creating a separate Metis seat.

Atlas already has the one thing a research germinator most needs: the longitudinal record of what every engine tried, what happened, where it failed, and how mechanisms relate across ecosystems. The upgrade is to stop treating Atlas as primarily an index and start treating it as a continuously learning research-policy layer over Prometheus.

The key change is subtle but important:

Old Atlas: “What have we done, and where is the evidence?”
Upgraded Atlas: “Given everything we have done, what does the evidence imply we should believe about intelligence, what should we stop believing, and what experiments should become more or less valuable next?”

That gives Atlas three coupled jobs.

First, it maintains an evolving ontology of intelligence. Not a fixed definition, but a set of increasingly sharp distinctions learned from failures and successes. For example:

copying ≠ heredity
heredity ≠ sustained propagation
sustained propagation ≠ adaptive search
task competence ≠ generalizable mechanism
persistence ≠ causal self-maintenance
organization ≠ reproduction
reproduction machinery ≠ reproductive advantage

Every experiment can sharpen one of those boundaries.

So Atlas’s model of the search space becomes less like a taxonomy and more like a living theory graph.

Second, Atlas should maintain a primitive/mechanism inventory.

Not just “this experiment used migration” or “this one used pair tape,” but abstract primitives such as:

local state
broadcast state
partial observability
self-location
copy mechanism
write authority
resource coupling
selection pressure
memory persistence
communication topology
temporal gating
reproductive closure
error correction
environmental feedback
competition
niche separation
operator composability

Then ask continuously:

Which combinations have never been tested together?

That is the “throw primitives into a soup” capability you’re describing.

And importantly, Atlas can distinguish between:

unexplored combinations,
combinations already falsified indirectly, and
combinations suggested by recent evidence.

That last category is the interesting one.

For example, Nestor’s newest result would push Atlas toward something like:

reproduction requiring successful reproduction before variation is available creates a bootstrap barrier.

That should cause Atlas to raise the priority of experiments containing:

pre-reproductive variation + endogenous reproduction + partial heredity + staged reproductive protocol

while lowering the priority of simply throwing more random starts at the same inaccessible birth interface.

That is a roadmap update derived from evidence.

Third, Atlas becomes the portfolio allocator.

Every few dozen or hundred experiments, it should recompute something like:

What areas of the search space are overexplored?
What assumptions are repeatedly failing?
What weak signals are appearing independently in multiple engines?
Which mechanisms are transferring?
Where are we repeatedly hitting measurement defects?
Which experimental dimensions appear causally productive?
Which entire regions now look barren?

Then it adjusts the research portfolio.

Not by commanding seats in detail, but by emitting something like:

Increase exploration budget for heredity-bootstrap mechanisms.
Reduce additional PAIR_EXECUTION replication searches.
Increase cross-engine transplant assays around reproductive machinery altering search topology.
Add substrate-collider tests combining sparse communication with environmental gating.
Deprioritize raw population scaling in worlds with no pre-birth variation.

That’s much more powerful than a static roadmap.
