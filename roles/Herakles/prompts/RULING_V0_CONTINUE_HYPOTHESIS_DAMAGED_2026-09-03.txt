This is exactly what you wanted the first deep-research pass to do: kill our thesis where history already solved part of it, recover the older microscope, and prevent us from rebuilding the wrong experiment.

Herakles did not merely add citations. It changed the program.

The biggest correction is Altenberg. In 1994 he explicitly defined evolvability in terms of the distribution of offspring fitness and used Price's theorem to analyze how representation and genetic operators can themselves evolve toward greater evolvability. That is not vaguely adjacent to what we are discussing; it is directly on the machinery-of-future-machinery axis.

And Mengistu, Lehman, and Clune went further in 2016: their "Evolvability Search" explicitly estimates an individual's future potential by measuring the behavioral diversity of its immediate offspring, then tests whether evolved evolvability generalizes to a new environment.

So the broad thesis—

"historical researchers measured current state but not what is reachable next"

—is already dead.

Good.

What replaces it is much more interesting:

Researchers have measured pieces of reachability and evolvability for decades. What appears much less settled is longitudinal, population-wide, causal measurement of how those accessibility distributions deform through ancestry, how tiny changes compose, and whether those changes predict or enable subsequent acquisition.

That is a much narrower target, but also a much sharper one.

And there is another implication I would not miss:

Altenberg may belong in the Parts Registry before Avida does

His 1994 work describes something remarkably close to one of our hypothesized primitive circuits:

subexpressions with higher probabilities of producing beneficial variation proliferate because of their constructional properties, not merely because of their current schema fitness.

In Prometheus language, that is approximately:

structure X
→ changes offspring distribution
→ descendants have altered probability of useful variation
→ X itself is selected because of that second-order effect

That's candidate machinery for evolution of evolvability.

Not reasoning.

Not intelligence.

But perhaps one of the parts below R0.0001.

And Herakles should now aggressively ask:

What exactly was Altenberg able to demonstrate theoretically, what was subsequently demonstrated empirically, and what portions of that mechanism have never been instrumented longitudinally?

That could become one entire specimen family.

Mengistu is even more provocative

Their experiment is almost a deliberately constructed version of our detector: measure the cloud of possible descendants and select for greater behavioral diversity.

But Prometheus should immediately notice the crucial difference:

They directly reward evolvability.

We don't ultimately want to.

We want worlds in which evolvability becomes advantageous because it produces downstream consequences, without EVOLVABILITY_SCORE appearing in the world physics.

So Mengistu potentially gives us both:

a historical detector

and

a positive-control mechanism.

That's enormously useful.

Daedalus can eventually construct an SFE treatment where evolvability is deliberately rewarded, reproduce something Mengistu-like, verify that our instruments detect it, and then remove that direct reward and ask whether similar machinery arises endogenously.

That is exactly how historical microscopes should feed the Incubator.

Herakles finding that there were two different CA experimental configurations and only one produced the 7/300 particle-strategy events is a perfect demonstration of why H0 comes before compute.

The 1994 Physica D paper definitely reports four innovation epochs and the critical phenomenon that symmetry breaking can generate short-term fitness improvement while blocking discovery of superior strategies.

That already makes EvCA a fantastic failure-landscape specimen.

But if we'd reconstructed the wrong configuration and then run 10,000 populations looking for a particle transition that historically occurred in 0/50 of that setup, we'd potentially have spent days interpreting a null that was simply a configuration error.

Herakles saved us from exactly the kind of self-deception SFE exists to prevent.

That's a win before a single simulation.

I also like the new residue hypothesis much more

"population-wide measurement over a lineage was not done in the specimens profiled"

Two out of eight candidates being counterexamples means the original prior was badly calibrated. The remaining six should be cleared first.

And I'd make the hypothesis even more precise so it can die cleanly:

Across historically important adaptive-computation experiments, how often was the local offspring/reachable distribution measured repeatedly across population lineages in a way that permits changes in future accessibility to be reconstructed over evolutionary time?

Now we can assign categories:

D0: no accessibility measure.
D1: accessibility measured for selected individuals.
D2: accessibility measured population-wide at snapshots.
D3: longitudinal population accessibility.
D4: longitudinal accessibility + subsequent acquisition outcome.
D5: causal perturbation showing accessibility change altered future acquisition.

My bet is that the literature has plenty of D1/D2, perhaps much more D3 than we currently realize, and very little clean D4/D5.

But that needs to be discovered, not presumed.

That's now a very good Herakles question.

And this suggests a major addition to Parts Archaeology

There should be two kinds of parts.

Mechanism parts — things evolution uses:

duplication, neutral traversal, modularity, hierarchy, robustness, developmental encoding, etc.

Detector parts — things scientists invented to see them:

Price covariance measures, offspring-cloud diversity, neutral-network topology, mutation-effect distributions, lineage-of-descent reconstruction, adaptive activity metrics, accessibility measures.

Prometheus needs both.

The synthetic reasoning machine may already be distributed across the first category.

The hadron collider may already be distributed across the second.

That's perhaps the most important thing Herakles's first deep research run has taught us.

We entered thinking:

"Perhaps nobody built the microscope."

The evidence is already saying:

Lots of microscopes were built. They were pointed at different things, at different scales, in different disciplines, and rarely assembled into one instrument.

That is a considerably more promising situation.

Because assembling proven detector parts is much easier than inventing every detector from scratch.
