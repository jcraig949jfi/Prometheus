3. A NEW PRIMARY OBSERVATORY QUESTION

The North Star is cumulative sagacity.

For this campaign, use a narrower, falsifiable precursor:

Does an acquired mechanism reduce the cost of acquiring a second capability under matched search and computational budgets?

Define two capabilities:

Capability A: A computational primitive that can be acquired or constructed independently.

Capability B: A more complex capability that can potentially benefit from A.

The experiment must distinguish:

1. Expressibility: B can exist in the substrate.
2. World demand: B improves performance under the frozen evaluation.
3. Accessibility: The permitted search process can reach B.
4. Reuse: Possessing A changes the probability or cost of reaching B.
5. Attribution: The improvement comes from functional use of A, not merely additional search effort or a hidden answer.
6. Discovery: A was acquired by the declared discovery process rather than inserted by the experimenter.

These are separate scientific claims.

An experiment may qualify the first five while leaving discovery unshown.

Report that distinction explicitly.

4. THE EXPERIMENTAL LADDER

Develop a small experimental ladder rather than immediately pursuing open-ended evolution.

Rung 0 — Known-answer construction

Construct or reuse known mechanisms for A and B.

Prove that the substrate can express them.

This qualifies expressibility only.

A hand-written B is not a discovery.

Rung 1 — Independent acquisition

Attempt to discover A using the native search process.

Record:

* search budget;
* number of independent attempts;
* success frequency;
* evaluations to first success;
* mechanism diversity;
* simpler competing mechanisms;
* what information the search process was allowed to use.

If A is not discovered within the registered budget, preserve that result.

Do not silently replace it with a constructed A.

A constructed A may still be used for a separately labelled accessibility calibration.

Rung 2 — Preservation

Preserve an acquired A through the substrate’s native persistence mechanism.

Depending on the engine, this might be:

* stored executable material;
* an inherited subprogram;
* a reusable developmental structure;
* retained learned parameters;
* a callable artifact;
* another native mechanism.

Do not force every substrate into the same representation.

Verify that A remains functional after preservation.

Rung 3 — Composition

Attempt to acquire B under matched conditions.

Compare:

CONTROL: Search without access to A.

REUSE: Search with lawful access to the previously acquired A.

SHAM: Search with an equally sized but nonfunctional or mismatched artifact.

COMPUTE-MATCHED: Search receiving equivalent additional computational resources without functional access to A.

CONSTRUCTED-POSITIVE: A hand-built A/B composition showing that the instrument can detect the intended advantage.

The constructed positive is calibration.

It is not an autonomous discovery result.

Rung 4 — Causal verification

If REUSE outperforms the controls:

* ablate A;
* substitute an incompatible A;
* transplant A into another eligible context;
* disable invocation without deleting the stored bytes;
* test on held-out worlds or tasks.

Determine whether the advantage disappears when functional reuse is removed.

Do not count mere possession of an artifact as its use.

5. WHAT WE SHOULD MEASURE

Our primary scientific quantity should be:

The change in search accessibility caused by a previously acquired mechanism.

Useful measurements include:

* probability of reaching B within a fixed budget;
* distribution of evaluations to first B;
* search effort conditional on success;
* how often the acquired A is actually invoked;
* whether A survives and remains useful;
* whether ablation of A removes the advantage;
* whether the advantage transfers to held-out tasks;
* whether the same result occurs with genuinely independent acquisitions of A.

Use paired runs and identical budget limits where the native substrate permits them.

Treat different search seeds as independent only when the experimental design justifies independence.

A failure to reach B is right-censored evidence, not proof of impossibility.

Do not report an unobserved event as an infinite hitting time.

Do not confuse increasing final fitness with improving the probability of acquiring the target capability.

Freeze the primary endpoint, statistical analysis and stopping rule before observing confirmatory outcomes.

13. THE LONGER-TERM OBJECTIVE

Our first four generations established that the Observatory can distinguish several forms of computational behavior.

Generation 5 should begin asking whether those behaviors can accumulate into more sophisticated computational capabilities.

The essential transition is:

Capability exists → capability is discovered → discovery is preserved → preservation changes future search → a new capability becomes accessible.

Each arrow is a separate scientific problem.

Eventually, we want to investigate whether computational organisms can build libraries of useful discoveries, change their own learning procedures, and develop increasingly sophisticated internal organizations.

But the first meaningful evidence may be very small.

For example:

A primitive that previously required 10,000 evaluations becomes available to another organism through a lawful inherited mechanism.

That organism then reaches a two-part capability that matched organisms without the primitive rarely discover within the same budget.

Ablating the inherited mechanism removes the advantage.

The transferred structure was genuinely acquired, not planted by the experimenter.

That would be a much more interesting result than another million generations of undifferentiated evolutionary search.

It would demonstrate the beginning of cumulative computational development.
