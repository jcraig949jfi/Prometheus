Horizon I — Immediate scientific qualification

Hours to days.

Their objectives include:

* proving that a runtime behaves as specified;
* calibrating rulers;
* discovering implementation defects;
* establishing whether a proposed mechanism is expressible;
* measuring small-scale search accessibility;
* rejecting simpler explanations.

Horizon II — Developmental and evolutionary exploration

Days to weeks, potentially longer when justified.

* whether a useful primitive can emerge;
* whether it survives;
* whether it becomes reusable;
* whether a second primitive can build upon it;
* whether learned abstractions reduce later acquisition costs;
* whether worlds and organisms can co-develop increasingly sophisticated capabilities;
* whether previously inaccessible mechanisms become reachable through developmental changes.

Weeks, months, and eventually longer.

We want to investigate systems whose capabilities evolve across several interacting timescales:

1. Learning and adaptation during an organism’s lifetime.
2. Consolidation and reuse of discoveries across tasks or developmental stages.
3. Evolution of the machinery that performs learning, consolidation, and abstraction.

At this horizon, we may study entire populations and ecologies in which new capabilities alter subsequent evolutionary opportunities.

We may eventually investigate whether the process becomes more effective at generating novel, useful cognitive mechanisms over time.

A long-running simulation is not automatically an experiment in open-ended intelligence.

18. The North Star

I want you to carry one distinction through every architectural recommendation.

Optimizing an existing capability is not the same as evolving the ability to acquire new capabilities.

We are not trying to evolve increasingly elaborate calculators.

We are trying to understand whether systems can evolve the capacity to discover, preserve, combine, revise, and extend useful knowledge.

The most interesting future Prometheus organism may not be the one with the highest score.

It may be the one that makes a discovery that changes what its descendants can learn.

Or the one that discovers a new internal representation that compresses an otherwise enormous search problem.

Or the one that develops a learning procedure that makes formerly inaccessible mechanisms commonplace.

Or the one whose understanding of a world improves its ability to discover the next rule governing that world.

And we must remain open to discovering that none of our current representations can produce these behaviors.

We cannot purchase a frontier laboratory’s compute infrastructure.

But we can design experiments that ask better questions, search more intelligently, preserve discoveries, and learn systematically from failures.

Prometheus must become disproportionately powerful relative to the resources available to it.

That is an engineering requirement, a scientific requirement, and an architectural challenge.
