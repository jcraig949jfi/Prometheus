Two campaigns finished in one pass: AETH-02 closed with four zero-dollar
falsifiers, and the RunPod engineering ladder flew Iteration 1 on real

## What happened

The AETH-02 trajectories landed against their dollar ceiling at $2.608,
completing two of three 50,000-tick worlds and truncating the third at
~47,000 — exactly as predicted from the live throughput, because my
preregistered cost was 7.2% low. Wrote the trajectory report, then ran
H1–H4 on CPU for nothing.

Then the operator made the GPU platform the primary mission, and Iteration
1 took six attempts to pass. Five failures, $0.121, and every one of them
found something the fake provider could not have.

## The three things worth remembering

**I reported an identity as a validation.** The trajectory report claimed
an "observer consistency check": `changed_by_field` equals `STATE_CHANGING`
exactly at every sample of every phase. It does — because `edge_aggregates`
computes `(has & differs).sum()` and `edge_classes` computes
`(has & changed).sum()` from the same inputs. They cannot disagree. I
looked at an exact 1.0000 ratio across hundreds of samples and called it
evidence instead of asking why it could not have come out otherwise. An
exact agreement is the weakest possible evidence, because the likeliest
explanation is that you have measured one thing twice.

Finding that led to the real defect underneath it: `aeth02_runner.py`
refreshes its `prev` snapshot only when it emits a sample, so every
comparison was against the state 250 ticks earlier. I had read
`change_rate(xp, prev, device)`, recognised the right function, and never
checked what `prev` held. **Verify the operand, not just the operation.**
A variable refreshed on a different cadence from its use is a trap, and
the name `prev` actively helped me not see it.

I measured the bias rather than arguing about it — same lattice, same
tick, same observer, only the reference state differing — which also
revealed that the "control failure" I had been worrying about was the same
mistake in the other direction. The corrected numbers move every
conclusion slightly further in the direction already reported. That is
luck, not vindication, and I want to record it as luck.

**Marking a number INFERRED is not the same as measuring it.** I put
`provision` and `bootstrap` in the cost model as inferred, with provenance
strings saying so, and felt I had been careful. Nothing forced them to be
measured. They propagated into COST_MODEL.md, into every projection, and
into a test asserting that overhead exceeds 50% of a one-minute job.
Iteration 1 measured the total at ~35 s against the model's 78 s — and the
better measurement **failed my own test**, because the test had encoded an
estimate as a property. A test pinned to a figure it did not derive fails
exactly when the figure improves. `provision` is now recorded as a bound
(9–24 s), not a value, because 15 s of the interval is my own poll
granularity and I would rather publish a range than a number I cannot
defend.

**Silence is the most expensive failure mode.** Iteration 1's second
attempt spent $0.044 and ten minutes to report "pod never reported ready".
The third attempt, with a failed fetch recording url, status and error,
identified the cause in its first second: `404`, which meant the artifact
server had been up the whole time and I was asking for the wrong path. The
contract said artifact paths are relative to the artifact directory; the
code and both examples said `out/`; and the conformance test **stripped the
prefix**, converting the defect into a passing suite. A test that
normalises away a discrepancy is worse than no test.

## What went right

The controller's safety properties all held under real conditions I had
not anticipated. Three creates refused for capacity were reconciled and
reported as a clean non-event with nothing billing. A pod that never became
reachable was still terminated, confirmed absent, and given a receipt. Six
attempts, six clean teardowns, zero leaks, and the inventory independently
verified empty after each. The fake provider earned its keep: it could not
produce these conditions, but once each was understood it took minutes to
encode as a test, so none of them can come back.

Flying cost $0.124 and bought four platform capabilities I would not have
designed unprompted: GPU alternatives, an artifact server, progress-based
waiting, and fetch diagnostics. That is the argument for flying early on a
small budget rather than reasoning longer.
